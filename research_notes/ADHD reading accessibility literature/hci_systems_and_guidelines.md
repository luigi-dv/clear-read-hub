# HCI systems, user studies and guidelines for reading by people with ADHD and other cognitive/attention-related disabilities

Research method note: all findings below were gathered via web search summaries and snippet text (searches run 2026-10-20). Direct page fetches of dl.acm.org, w3.org, arxiv.org, api.crossref.org, api.semanticscholar.org, commonlit.org and accessibility.blog.gov.uk were refused by the session's egress proxy, and the per-turn web-search budget was exhausted before every planned query could run. Consequently (a) abstracts and metadata were verified from search-result snippets rather than full texts, (b) a few well-known references that I could not verify in this session are explicitly labelled "NOT VERIFIED IN THIS SESSION" and should be checked before use, and (c) several gaps are listed at the end of each section. Every item that is listed as a cited finding has a URL or DOI that the search engine returned.

Verification status legend used throughout:

## Key Question 1: Which CHI / ASSETS / TACCESS / W4A / CSCW (etc.) papers from ~2015–2026 study ADHD adults or students and reading, studying, note-taking, focus or document consumption, and what did they find?

### Takeaway
The HCI/accessibility literature on ADHD has grown sharply since ~2022 but remains thin on *reading and document consumption* specifically: only two 2026 papers (a CHI EA pupillometry/typography study with 21 readers, 6 with ADHD; and a Creativity & Cognition paper restructuring LLM output for 10 users with attention challenges) directly test reading interfaces, while most ADHD HCI work targets task management, focus/body doubling, video learning, or critiques of neuro-normative design. Consistent design recommendations across these studies are: chunked text with clear sectioning, reduced visual clutter and intrusive scrolling, user-controlled customisation with a small number of controls (because too many options become a distraction), visual hierarchy that supports scanning rather than mere shortening, and preserving user agency (original/adapted toggles).

### Cited Findings

**Direct reading / text-presentation studies with ADHD participants**

- "Reading with Diversity in Mind: Pupillometry and Typography Towards Inclusive Design for ADHD Readers" — Borano Llana, Alisa Baron, Haihan Yu, Maedeh Hosseinpour, Yusra Suhail, Sean Chin, Kushas Khadka, Shaun Wallace (University of Rhode Island); CHI EA '26 (Extended Abstracts), DOI 10.1145/3772363.3799383 [peer-reviewed, extended abstract]. In-lab eye-tracking + pupillometry study with 21 participants, 6 with ADHD; participants read passages in four Roboto variants at three sizes and answered comprehension quizzes; follow-up interviews with ADHD participants about what helps/hurts focus. Mixed linear models showed a large effect size of ADHD status on reading speed; reading speed correlated negatively with mean pupil dilation (r = -0.376, p < .001). Recommendations: chunked text, clear sectioning, avoiding intrusive scrolling and visual clutter. Open-source tool "ReadGen" released for generating consistent text images for pupillometry experiments. — [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/full/10.1145/3772363.3799383); [a11y-paradise summary (secondary)](https://a11y-paradise.onrender.com/reviews/69ed2c3f083d4508fd230b16)
  - Preliminary 3-participant VSS 2025 poster by the same group found no relationship between font size and pupil size, nor between font-style variations within a typeface and concentration (weak evidence). — [VSS 2025](https://visionsciences.org/presentation/?id=2470)

- "Too Long to Read? Restructuring LLM Responses for Users with Attention-Related Challenges" — Jaeyeon Lee, Seo Yeon Lee, Gyuyeon Jung, Morgan G. Ames; ACM Creativity and Cognition (C&C '26), pp. 1605–1609, DOI 10.1145/3803784.3816862 [peer-reviewed, short paper]. Built "Adapt AI", which restructures LLM responses via content condensation, visual hierarchy and an original/adapted toggle, intervening at the presentation layer rather than the model. Interview study with 10 participants with attention, working-memory and information-processing challenges: all 10 said the adapted view was easier to read; 7/10 spontaneously described it as making AI content more accessible. Authors caveat: no comparison group, demand characteristics. Conclusion: benefit comes from reorganising for scanning/comprehension/agency, not from shortening; appropriate level of restructuring depends on task. — [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3803784.3816862)

- "Pay attention to digital text: The impact of the media on text comprehension and self-monitoring in higher-education students with ADHD" — Gal Ben-Yehudah & Adi Brann; Research in Developmental Disabilities 89:120–129 (2019), DOI 10.1016/j.ridd.2019.04.001 [peer-reviewed journal, not HCI venue]. 45 students with ADHD and 61 matched controls read an expository text on screen or on paper. ADHD group showed a larger digital-text disadvantage; self-monitoring (prediction of own performance) was worse/overconfident for ADHD readers only in the digital condition; in print, predictions and scores were similar across groups though ADHD readers spent more time. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0891422219300605); [Open University of Israel author page](https://www.openu.ac.il/en/personalsites/adibrann.aspx)
  - Earlier related work: Stern et al. (2013), Research in Developmental Disabilities — adolescents with/without ADHD read single- vs double-spaced passages on paper vs screen with a sustained-attention test; per a secondary summary, double-spacing and screen text improved comprehension only for teens with good sustained attention, not for those with weak attention. Co-author list not confirmed from primary source. — [Behaviorist Book Club summary (secondary)](https://behavioristbookclub.com/aba-research/assessment-research/the-role-of-sustained-attention-and-display-medium-in-reading-comprehe/); related 2012 J. Vision abstract listed at [TAU attention lab](https://attention.tau.ac.il/publications/published-proceedings/)
  - A 2023 Open University of Israel study (Segal) found sustained attention was the only cognitive measure predicting reading comprehension in adults with and without ADHD. — [OUI CRIS](https://cris.openu.ac.il/en/publications/sustained-attention-plays-a-critical-role-in-reading-comprehensio/)

**Video / lecture consumption (closest analogue to document consumption)**

- "FocusView: Understanding and Customizing Informational Video Watching Experiences for Viewers with ADHD" — Hanxiu "Hazel" Zhu, Ruijia Chen, Yuhang Zhao; ASSETS '25, DOI 10.1145/3663547.3746386; arXiv 2507.13309 / 2511.01248 [peer-reviewed]. System segments video into visual and auditory elements and allows customisation of layout, background, caption and audio. Evaluated with 12 participants with ADHD: perceived viewability improved significantly (F = 165.4, p < 0.001, η²p = 0.75); effectiveness rating M = 6.17 (SD 0.78), ease of use M = 6.67 (SD 0.44), customisation effort M = 1.54 (SD 0.66). Perceptions of distraction varied (background music distracting for some, stimulating for others). Design tension: participants liked the limited option set but wanted more targeted controls; authors warn too many controls make customisation itself a distraction. A third-party review notes the headline effect may rest on a confounded first-half/second-half design (unverified). — [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3663547.3746386); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.13309)

- "'It's Like Drinking from a Fire Hose': Understanding and Characterizing Video Learning Experiences for Individuals with ADHD" — arXiv 2609.09443 (2026) [preprint; author list not retrieved]. Retrospective think-aloud study with 16 participants with ADHD; identified lecture-design elements that hinder learning (e.g., overly dense visuals) linked to observable behaviours (misaligned gaze, prolonged fixations); coping strategies included "attention reboot" on secondary visuals when understimulated and using AI tools outside the video to restructure inaccessible content. Aim: inform adaptive, personalised video learning design. — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.09443)

**Studying, academic work and task management (ADHD students / adults)**

- "Navigating STEM Doctoral Programs with ADHD: Barriers, Workflow Challenges, and Adaptive Strategies" — Paul Ezeamii & Kristen Shinohara (RIT); ASSETS '25 (Denver, Oct 2025), DOI 10.1145/3663547.3746325 [peer-reviewed]. Two-part mixed-methods design: semi-structured interviews with 5 current/recent PhD students with ADHD, then experience sampling + contextual inquiry with 9 STEM PhD students (ACM listing says 13 total; the 5+9 breakdown may overlap—check full text). Examines institutional barriers, workflow challenges, and the view that traits such as hyperfocus can be strengths that academic systems fail to support. — [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/full/10.1145/3663547.3746325); [Shinohara CV listing as C27](https://kristenshinohara.com/cv_shinohara.pdf)

- "Beyond Individual Accommodations: The Collaborative Practices of ADHD Students in Post-Secondary Education" — Anne Marie Piper (UC Irvine) and co-authors; ASSETS '25, Article 36, pp. 1–14, DOI 10.1145/3663547.3746324 [peer-reviewed]. Analysis of posts from a Reddit community of post-secondary ADHD students. Identifies three collaborative practices: body doubling for focus/accountability; active collaborative support for managing emotions and distractions; community-based support for deadlines and belonging. Frames access as shaped by social practices and the material environment. — [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3663547.3746324)

- "'Not Just Me and My To-Do List': Understanding Challenges of Task Management for Adults with ADHD and the Need for AI-Augmented Social Scaffolds" — Jingruo Chen, Yibo Meng, Kexin Nie; accepted to CSCW 2026; ACM DOI 10.1145/3816978; arXiv 2603.17258 (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0) [peer-reviewed; arXiv version]. 22 semi-structured interviews with adults with ADHD, then a speed-dating study with 20 further adults reacting to 13 speculative AI concepts. Finding: task management is relational and emotional, not solitary; recommends socially aware AI supporting co-regulation and non-linear attention patterns. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17258); [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3816978)

- "ADHD and Knowledge Work: Exploring Strategies, Challenges and Opportunities for AI" — Jennifer Campbell, Sena Çerçi, Marta E. Cecchinato (Northumbria); INTERACT 2023 (IFIP TC13), Springer LNCS, DOI 10.1007/978-3-031-61688-4_10 (published 2024) [peer-reviewed]. Online survey, n = 49 adults with ADHD on managing work. Strategies/tools still leave difficulties with prioritisation, time estimation and task switching; some wanted AI-powered tools, awareness/use limited, but users found them very helpful. Recommendations: raise awareness of existing tools, co-design inclusive tools, manager/colleague support. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-031-61688-4_10); [Northumbria portal](https://researchportal.northumbria.ac.uk/en/publications/adhd-and-knowledge-work-exploring-strategies-challenges-and-oppor/)

- Classroom note-taking study (not HCI venue): Shimko, Fyfe & James (2025), Applied Cognitive Psychology, DOI 10.1002/acp.70105 [peer-reviewed]. n = 137 students; as inattentive ADHD symptoms rose, students who chose laptops/tablets scored better on a quiz while handwriters scored worse; handwriters reported less distraction. — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/acp.70105?af=R)

**Critical reviews / framing papers**

- "ADHD and Technology Research – Investigated by Neurodivergent Readers" — Katta Spiel, Eva Hornecker, Rua Mae Williams, Judith Good; CHI '22, pp. 1–21, DOI 10.1145/3491102.3517592 [peer-reviewed]. Critical literature review of ADHD technologies written from an insider (neurodivergent-researcher) perspective. Findings: technologies are largely used to "mitigate" ADHD experiences seen as disruptive to neurotypical norms; little HCI research invites people with ADHD to co-design; research aims favouring neuro-normative outcomes create an epistemic imbalance. Number of papers reviewed not retrieved. — [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3491102.3517592); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/360330913_ADHD_and_Technology_Research_-_Investigated_by_Neurodivergent_Readers)

- "Preliminary Results of a Scoping Review on Assistive Technologies for Adults with ADHD" — Valerie Tan, Luisa Jost, Jens Gerken, Max Pascher (TU Dortmund); AT@WORK workshop co-located with ASSETS 2025 (workshop, not main track); arXiv 2601.21791, submitted 29 Jan 2026 [preprint/workshop]. Most adopt a therapeutic/intervention lens rather than a positive support lens; sharp rise in recent adult-ADHD publications. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.21791v1)

- A separate, larger scoping review: "Digital health technologies for adults with ADHD" (Frontiers in Digital Health, Feb 2026; 133 eligible studies, search to Dec 2025). — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/digital-health/articles/10.3389/fdgth.2026.1746732/full)

- "'You Can't Possibly Have ADHD': Exploring Validation and Tensions around Diagnosis within Unbounded ADHD Social Media Communities" — Tessa Eagle & Kathryn E. (Not reading-specific.) — [SIGACCESS newsletter](https://www.sigaccess.org/newsletter/2023-01/frontmatter.html)

- "Proposing body doubling as a continuum of space/time and mutuality: An investigation with neurodivergent participants" — T. Eagle, L. B. Baltaxe-Admony, K. E. - "'I Am Human, Just Like You': What Intersectional, Neurodivergent Lived Experiences Bring to Accessibility Research" — Lindy Le; ASSETS '24; arXiv 2408.04500 [peer-reviewed + preprint]. (Positionality/autoethnographic; not reading-specific.) — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.04500)

- "Co-Designing Programmable Fidgeting Experience with Swarm Robots for Adults with ADHD" — Pulatova & Kim; ASSETS '24 [peer-reviewed] (focus aid, not reading). — [ASSETS '24 programme](https://assets24.sigaccess.org/program/schedule/)

**LLM use by neurodivergent people (document distillation as a use case)**

- "Exploring Large Language Models Through a Neurodivergent Lens: Use, Challenges, Community-Driven Workarounds, and Concerns" — ACM (Proc. ACM HCI / CSCW), DOI 10.1145/3701194; arXiv 2410.06336 (2024) [peer-reviewed; author list not retrieved in this session—Virginia Tech affiliation per VTechWorks copy]. Qualitative analysis of 61 neurodivergent Reddit communities; 20 LLM use cases across five areas (emotional well-being, mental-health support, interpersonal communication, learning, professional development); many ADHD users use LLMs to distil long conversations/texts; main challenges: overly neurotypical responses, limits of text-based interaction; concerns: over-reliance, replacing human connection. — [ACM DL PDF](https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/3701194); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.06336v1); [VTechWorks](https://vtechworks.lib.vt.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/b88bc9d1-4711-457e-a836-b8b8f96d1963/content)

- "Understanding Human-AI Misalignment in LLM-Based Job-Seeking Support for Neurodivergent Users" — ASSETS '25, DOI 10.1145/3663547.3746361 [peer-reviewed]. Interviews with 15 neurodivergent participants: chatbot misrepresented skills, imposed neurotypical language/expectations, gave generic advice. — [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/full/10.1145/3663547.3746361)

### Inferences
- Across the two direct reading-interface studies (Llana et al. 2026; Lee et al. 2026) and the FocusView video study, the convergent design pattern is "structure, don't just shorten": chunking, visual hierarchy, clear sectioning, a small set of user-controlled toggles, and an always-available original view. These are small-sample, mostly uncontrolled studies, so the recommendations are design hypotheses rather than established effects.
- Ben-Yehudah & Brann (2019) and the Stern et al. (2013) line suggest that for ADHD readers the *medium* (screen vs print) and the reader's sustained-attention capacity interact with text format; typographic changes alone (spacing) may only help readers with adequate sustained attention. This argues for adaptive interfaces that also support self-monitoring/metacognition, not just presentation changes.
- The field is dominated by qualitative, small-n ASSETS/CHI work and critical reviews (Spiel et al. 2022; Tan et al. 2026) that warn against "deficit-mitigation" framings; any new reading tool for ADHD would be expected by this community to be co-designed with ADHD users and to frame features as support/preference rather than correction.

### Gaps
- I could not locate any CHI/ASSETS/TACCESS/W4A/CSCW paper by Elizabeth Kaziunas, Sarah Pfister, Kyle Zhang, Tamanna Motahar or Anya Kolesnichenko specifically on ADHD and reading; the search budget ran out before author-specific queries completed. Their involvement is unverified; do not cite them for ADHD-reading work without checking.
- A survey titled "ADHD and self-regulation technologies" was not found; the closest verified reviews are Spiel et al. (CHI '22), Tan et al. (AT@WORK 2025/arXiv 2026) and the Frontiers Digital Health 2026 scoping review.
- The ASSETS 2026 accepted-papers list (assets26.sigaccess.org) could not be fetched; the search summary mentioned "a paper on adult students with ADHD is listed for ASSETS 2026" but gave no title or authors.
- Full texts were not accessible, so exact design-recommendation lists (e.g., from Ezeamii & Shinohara, Piper et al.) are not enumerated here.
- The 2024 ACM TACCESS body-doubling paper by Ringland and colleagues is cited in the 2026 roadmap paper but I did not retrieve its title/DOI.

## Key Question 2: What does W3C publish on cognitive accessibility of text (WCAG 2.2 SCs 1.4.8, 1.4.12, 3.1.5; COGA "Making Content Usable"), including ADHD personas/objectives, and what is the evidence base?

### Takeaway
WCAG 2.2 addresses text readability mainly through SC 1.4.12 Text Spacing (AA; content must survive user-set line height 1.5×, paragraph spacing 2×, letter spacing 0.12 em, word spacing 0.16 em) and the AAA criteria 1.4.8 Visual Presentation and 3.1.5 Reading Level (supplement text above lower-secondary reading level); the COGA Task Force's non-normative Note "Making Content Usable for People with Cognitive and Learning Disabilities" (2021) adds eight objectives (notably "Use clear and understandable content", "Help users to maintain focus", "Support adaptation and personalization") with ADHD personas (Carolyn / "Yuki" in some drafts; Paula in the user-research document; Stefan in WAI "Stories of Web Users"). The evidence base is a W3C "Cognitive Accessibility Research Modules" document (early draft Note, Feb 2026) plus task-force usability-testing guidance; the W3C pages themselves could not be fetched in this session, so the figures below come from search snippets and secondary guides.

### Cited Findings

**WCAG 2.2 success criteria**

- SC 1.4.12 Text Spacing (Level AA): no loss of content or functionality when users set line height ≥ 1.5× font size, paragraph spacing ≥ 2× font size, letter spacing ≥ 0.12× font size, word spacing ≥ 0.16× font size. W3C's Understanding page frames the goal as letting users adjust spacing to make text easier to read because some people need different spacing or font characteristics. — [W3C Understanding 1.4.12](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/text-spacing.html); numeric values confirmed via [thewcag.com (secondary)](https://www.thewcag.com/criteria/1.4.12); Silktide (secondary) states spacing "breaks up content for people with cognitive impairments, making it less overwhelming" — [Silktide](https://silktide.com/accessibility-guide/the-wcag-standard/1-4/distinguishable/1-4-12-text-spacing/)
- SC 1.4.8 Visual Presentation (Level AAA): W3C Understanding page says some people with cognitive, language and learning disabilities lose their reading place when layout is hard to read, that people with some cognitive disabilities read more easily when word spacing is regular, and that reflowing text lets people with cognitive disabilities enlarge text without disorientation. Secondary sources disagree on the line-spacing figure (WebAIM checklist says "at least space-and-a-half"; some guides cite 1.5) — verify against the 2.2 text. — [W3C Understanding 1.4.8 (WCAG 2.1 page)](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/visual-presentation.html); [WCAG 2.0 Understanding](https://www.w3.org/TR/UNDERSTANDING-WCAG20/visual-audio-contrast-visual-presentation.html); [WebAIM checklist](https://webaim.org/standards/wcag/WCAG2Checklist.pdf)
- SC 3.1.5 Reading Level (Level AAA): when text requires reading ability more advanced than lower secondary education level (roughly grades 7–9), supplemental content or a version not requiring that level must be available. Secondary guides note readability formulas measure only sentence length/syllables and cannot confirm meaning is clear, so human review is still needed. — [pageperson.org (secondary)](https://pageperson.org/resources/cognitive-accessibility-standards-wcag/); [accessibility.build (secondary)](https://accessibility.build/guides/cognitive-accessibility)

**COGA "Making Content Usable for People with Cognitive and Learning Disabilities"**

- Published as a W3C Working Group Note (2021 version; editor's draft at w3c.github.io/coga/content-usable). Not required for WCAG conformance. Organised around eight objectives: (1) help users understand what things are and how to use them; (2) help users find what they need; (3) use clear and understandable content; (4) help users avoid mistakes or correct them; (5) help users maintain focus; (6) ensure processes do not rely on memory; (7) provide help and support; (8) support adaptation and personalization. — [W3C TR](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable); [Design Guide](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/design_guide.html); [editor's draft](https://w3c.github.io/coga/content-usable/)
- The Note's usability-testing section explicitly lists AD(H)D among groups to include in testing ("a specific learning disability such as dyslexia, dyscalculia, or AD(H)D"). — [Usability Testing section](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/usability_testing.html)
- ADHD personas: the COGA "Proposed Personas" page describes "Carolyn", a yoga teacher with ADHD, whose Scenario 1 is pulling key points from dense web pages and Scenario 3 is losing focus during tasks. — [COGA personas](https://w3c.github.io/coga/persona/). The 2020 Working Draft Appendix A maps Objective 5 (focus / restore context) and a "Distractions" row to patterns such as limiting interruptions and short critical paths, and lists Carolyn under "Focus" for the Clear Purpose pattern. — [Appendix A, WD 2020-12-11](https://www.w3.org/TR/2020/WD-coga-usable-20201211/appendix_a.html); [User Stories WD](https://www.w3.org/TR/2020/WD-coga-usable-20201211/user_stories.html). The current TR "Use Cases / Persona" page has a persona "Yuki" with ADHD and planning difficulties whose text mirrors Carolyn's — naming inconsistency across drafts; verify. — [Use cases page](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/use_cases.html)
- "Cognitive Accessibility User Research" (W3C TR) includes persona "Paula", a 19-year-old with ADHD presenting as predominantly inattentive. — [W3C coga-user-research](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-user-research/)
- WAI "Stories of Web Users" includes "Stefan", a student with ADHD and dyslexia. — [WAI user stories](https://www.w3.org/WAI/people-use-web/user-stories/)
- Evidence base: "Cognitive Accessibility Research Modules", W3C Group Note draft, editor Lisa Seeman-Horwitz, dated 5 Feb 2026 (First Public Working Draft announced Feb 2026; comments requested by 16 Feb 2026); explicitly lists ADHD among covered disabilities; status is an early draft that "intends to add more research". Published by APA WG with AG WG approval. — [W3C TR](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-research-modules/); [dated draft](https://www.w3.org/TR/2026/DNOTE-coga-research-modules-20260205); [announcement](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/public-review-announce/2026Feb/0002.html). A companion module on voice/conversational interfaces exists. — [coga-voice](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-voice/)
- W3C cognitive accessibility landing page. — [WAI cognitive](https://www.w3.org/WAI/cognitive/)
- Secondary overview of the Note's publication and intent. — [Accessing Higher Ground](https://accessinghigherground.org/new-w3c-wai-guidance-on-making-content-usable-for-people-with-cognitive-and-learning-disabilities/); [DigitalA11Y](https://www.digitala11y.com/navigating-the-world-of-cognitive-disabilities-and-cognitive-accessibility-coga/)

### Inferences
- Only one WCAG criterion that bears on reading (1.4.12) is at Level AA; the ones most relevant to ADHD readers (1.4.8 reflow/spacing/width; 3.1.5 reading level) are AAA and therefore rarely required by law (EN 301 549 and most procurement rules stop at AA). This means a reading tool that adapts presentation and reading level is implementing guidance that most mainstream content does not meet.
- COGA's evidence base is largely expert-consensus plus a research-modules document that is itself still an early draft in 2026; it does not cite controlled studies of ADHD readers for most patterns. Treat COGA patterns as expert guidance, not empirically validated interventions.

### Gaps
- The W3C Understanding pages for 1.4.8 and 3.1.5 (WCAG 2.2 editions) could not be fetched; the exact wording and the cited resources sections were not verified.
- The exact ADHD sub-section text and references of the Research Modules were not retrieved.
- I did not verify whether the 2021 Note's current Appendix A still lists Carolyn vs Yuki.

## Key Question 3: What other standards or guidance exist (ISO 24495 plain language, ISO 21801 cognitive accessibility, EN 301 549, Easy-to-Read, UK GDS posters, Microsoft inclusive design)?

### Takeaway
ISO 24495-1:2023 (plain language) and ISO 21801-1:2020 (cognitive accessibility, confirmed current in 2025) are the two ISO documents most relevant to adapting text; EN 301 549 handles cognition only through a functional performance statement (clause 4.2.10 "Usage with limited cognition, language or learning") because its authors could not find testable, widely accepted cognitive requirements; Inclusion Europe's "Information for all" Easy-to-Read standards (2009/2010, 38-page 2014 edition, 16 languages) are the de facto European easy-read rules; the UK Home Office/GDS "Dos and don'ts" posters (2016) have no ADHD poster but the 2018 follow-up set was tested with ADHD staff; Microsoft's Inclusive Design toolkit has a Cognition Guide (2023 era) and a Mental Health extension.

### Cited Findings

- ISO 24495-1:2023 "Plain language — Part 1: Governing principles and guidelines": published 2023; principles for writing plain-language documents for any author; applies only to text-based printed or digital material; excludes existing digital accessibility guidance and points authors instead to WCAG and EN 301 549; examples in English only. — [ISO page](https://committee.iso.org/standard/78907.html?browse=ics); [Accessible EU Centre summary](https://accessible-eu-centre.ec.europa.eu/content-corner/digital-library/iso-24495-12023-plain-language-part-1-governing-principles-and-guidelines_en); [EVS](https://www.evs.ee/en/iso-24495-1-2023)
- ISO 21801-1:2020 "Cognitive accessibility — Part 1: General guidelines": published Jan 2020; reviewed and confirmed 2025 (remains current); covers products, services and built environments; limited to cognitive aspects; states that failure to follow it "could lead to barriers that would prevent some potential users from being able to use the system at all"; 22 pages, paid. — [ISO page](https://www.iso.org/standard/71711.html?browse=tc); [SCC listing](https://scc-ccn.ca/standardsdb/standards/8168498)
- EN 301 549 (ETSI; V3.2.1, 2021): clause 4.2.10 titled "Usage with limited cognition, language or learning" (earlier V2.1.1 title: "Usage with limited cognition"); it is a functional performance statement, not a testable requirement; cognition handled via Annex B informative mapping of WCAG criteria. 2015 mailing-list posts by EN 301 549 author Michael Pluke explain the drafting group could not find widely accepted testable cognitive requirements. Canada adopted it as CAN/ASC-EN 301 549:2024 (identical to the 2021 edition). — [ETSI V2.1.1 PDF](https://www.etsi.org/deliver/etsi_en/301500_301599/301549/02.01.01_20/en_301549v020101a.pdf); [Accessible Canada clause 4](https://accessible.canada.ca/node/379); [Annex B](https://accessible.canada.ca/en-301-549-accessibility-requirements-ict-products-and-services-annex-b-informative-relationship); [Pluke 2015 post](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/public-cognitive-a11y-tf/2015Feb/0080.html)
- Inclusion Europe "Information for all: European standards for making information easy to read and understand": free to use; the EU Publications Office cites the 2010 guidelines as the basis of its Easy-to-Read content, available in 16 languages; 2014 edition is 38 pages organised into general, written, electronic, video and audio standards; not EU law but referenced by EU bodies. — [Inclusion Europe](https://www.inclusion-europe.eu/easy-to-read-standards-guidelines/); [EU Publications Office](https://op.europa.eu/en/web/webguide/plain-language-and-easy-to-read); [commbox copy](https://www.commbox.org/document/information-for-all-european-standards-for-making-information-easy-to-read-and-understand)
- UK Home Office Digital / GDS "Dos and don'ts on designing for accessibility" (Sept 2016): six posters — low vision, D/deaf and hard of hearing, dyslexia, motor disabilities, autistic spectrum, screen-reader users; no ADHD poster. A 2018 follow-up poster set was tested with staff who have dyslexia, ADHD and mild visual impairment. — [GDS accessibility blog (Sept 2016)](https://accessibility.blog.gov.uk/2016/09/02); [Home Office Digital 2018 update](https://hodigital.blog.gov.uk/2018/05/17/illustrating-the-importance-of-accessible-design/); [2016 update](https://hodigital.blog.gov.uk/2016/12/28/designing-for-accessibility-an-update-on-our-accessibility-posters)
- Microsoft Inclusive Design: 2015 toolkit principles (recognise exclusion; learn from diversity; solve for one, extend to many); later "Inclusive Design for Cognition" guidebook; 2023 keynote described new guidelines for recognising cognitive differences and the exclusions that follow from failing to recognise them; Mental Health extension (2024-ish) builds on the cognitive toolkit. — [Microsoft Cognition Guide](https://inclusive.microsoft.design/articles/inclusive-design-for-cognition-guidebook); [Microsoft Design article](https://microsoft.design/articles/inclusive-designs-next-wave-breaks-for-mental-health/); [axe-con talk (secondary)](https://www.deque.com/axe-con/?p=31082)

### Inferences
- None of these standards contains ADHD-specific, testable requirements for reading interfaces; they operate at the level of functional statements (EN 301 549), general guidelines (ISO 21801-1) or writing rules (ISO 24495-1, Easy-to-Read). A reading tool can claim alignment with them but cannot claim "conformance" in any testable sense except via WCAG mapping.

- Microsoft's Cognition Guide content (the actual guidelines) was not retrieved.

## Key Question 4: What computational readability work targets specific populations (dyslexia, cognitive disabilities, personalised readability) and what 2023–2026 LLM text-adaptation papers were evaluated with neurodivergent/disabled participants?

### Takeaway
Population-specific readability modelling is best established for dyslexia (Luz Rello's DysWebxia line: eye-tracking experiments with ~240 participants, ~120 dyslexic; findings that frequent words improve readability and short words improve understandability, that Arial/Helvetica/Courier/Verdana outperform OpenDyslexic, and a 2015 W4A ML model predicting dyslexia from eye-tracking) and for intellectual disability (Yaneva et al.'s EasyRead/LocalNews corpora and the EASIER system). Personalised readability modelling exists at the lexical level (Gooding & Tragut 2022 personalised word-complexity models). LLM-based adaptation has been evaluated at scale with general users (Google, n = 4,563, +3.9 pp comprehension) and in tiny samples with intellectually disabled (MTCAI, n = 5) and attention-challenged users (Adapt AI, n = 10); I found no LLM text-adaptation study with a diagnosed-ADHD sample and a controlled comparison.

### Cited Findings

**Dyslexia (Rello et al.)**

- DysWebxia text accessibility model — Luz Rello, PhD thesis, Universitat Pompeu Fabra 2014 (supervisors Ricardo Baeza-Yates, Horacio Saggion); combines HCI validation with NLP; built on more than 20 eye-tracking experiments with 240 participants (120 with dyslexia). DysWebxia 2.0 (W4A 2013 demo) combines presentation (font, spacing, layout) with automatic content simplification; recommendations integrated into Text4All web service and the IDEAL ebook reader. — [UPF repository](https://repositori.upf.edu/items/960d188c-0745-45fa-9e84-55f9c6973879); [W4A 2013 PDF](https://www.changedyslexia.org/publications/pdfs/2013-W4A-DysWebxia%20More%20Accessible%20Text.pdf); [Innovators Under 35 interview](https://www.innovatorsunder35.com/the-list/luz-rello/)

Within-subject eye-tracking, 48 participants with clinically confirmed dyslexia (ages 11–50), 12 texts each in a different font; OpenDyslexic was not easier to read; Arial, Helvetica, Courier, Verdana performed best. — [a11y-paradise summary (secondary)](https://a11y-paradise.onrender.com/reviews/69d25a338a41f1818e3d54ca); [BYU editing research (secondary)](https://editingresearch.byu.edu/2020/05/28/which-fonts-are-best-for-dyslexia/)

### Inferences
- The strongest population-specific readability evidence is for dyslexia (eye-tracking, n ≈ 48–240) and it concerns *presentation* (font, spacing) and *lexical* features (frequency, length) rather than discourse structure; there is no comparable empirically grounded "ADHD readability model".
- Personalised readability work (Gooding & Tragut; Wallace line) suggests reader-level models outperform population-level ones even for lexical difficulty; this supports a design where a tool learns per-user preferences rather than applying a fixed "ADHD profile".
- All LLM-adaptation studies with disabled participants to date are n ≤ 10 and uncontrolled; the one large RCT (Google, n = 4,563) is general-population and reports a modest ~4-point gain. There is a clear research gap for a controlled LLM-adaptation study with ADHD readers.

### Gaps
- Could not find any 2023–2026 LLM document-adaptation paper evaluated with a *diagnosed ADHD* sample under controlled conditions. The closest are Lee et al. 2026 (attention-related challenges, n = 10, no control) and the Reddit analysis (DOI 10.1145/3701194).
- No "readability for people with cognitive disabilities" dataset with human judgments from ADHD readers was found.
- Venue/year of Gooding & Tragut and of Wallace et al. TOCHI 2022 not verified in this session.

## Key Question 5: Which datasets/corpora carry human readability judgements usable for research (CLEAR, OneStopEnglish, WeeBit, Newsela, dyslexia corpora), and what are their licences?

### Takeaway
CLEAR (≈5,000 excerpts, teacher pairwise comparisons scored with Bradley–Terry, grades 3–12; GitHub scrosseye/CLEAR-Corpus) and OneStopEnglish (189 texts × 3 levels, CC BY-SA 4.0) are the openly usable English readability corpora; Newsela requires an academic affiliation, publication intent and an NDA; WeeBit's licence/availability was not found; dyslexia-specific eye-tracking datasets exist (ETDD70, Czech children; CopCo, Danish) but their licences were not confirmed. CLEAR's licence could not be confirmed from the sources retrieved (the CommonLit blog's licence field reads only "Restrictions for use or distribution of the Excerpt").

### Cited Findings

- CLEAR (CommonLit Ease of Readability) corpus — Scott A. Crossley et al., "A large-scaled corpus for assessing text readability", Behavior Research Methods 55:491–507 (2023; online March 2022), DOI 10.3758/s13428-022-01802-x [peer-reviewed]. ≈5,000 reading-passage excerpts for grades 3–12 with metadata (year, genre); readability criterion based on teachers' pairwise ratings of relative difficulty (Bradley–Terry); traditional indices supplied (Flesch Reading Ease, FK Grade, SMOG, New Dale-Chall, plus CommonLit measures); hosted at GitHub scrosseye/CLEAR-Corpus. ## Key Question 6: Which existing reading tools claimed to help ADHD readers have been evaluated (BeeLine Reader, Helperbird, Speechify, Microsoft Immersive Reader, Readwise/Bionic Reading), and what is the evidence?

### Takeaway
No tool has independent, peer-reviewed evidence of benefit for ADHD readers. BeeLine Reader has one vendor-independent controlled study (WestEd 2018, 530 seventh-graders, engagement/comprehension gains, not ADHD) and one peer-reviewed study (2022, grade 2–3 children, mixed/negative readability effects); Immersive Reader's evidence is small dyslexia studies and a vendor-commissioned ESSA Level 4 report; Bionic Reading was tested by Readwise on 2,074 readers with no speed benefit; Helperbird and Speechify offer only marketing claims and testimonials; the only ADHD-adult evidence for text-to-speech is a small qualitative study.

### Cited Findings

- BeeLine Reader [vendor]: site claims "remarkable results" for readers with dyslexia, ADHD or other LD and a Perlego trial where college students using BeeLine spent 69% more time reading (no source given). — [beelinereader.com](https://beelinereader.com/)
  - WestEd 2018 controlled study (funded by NewSchools Venture Fund; BeeLine provided no funding): 530 seventh-graders; engagement and comprehension gains; not ADHD-focused; not a clinical trial. — [RIF-hosted WestEd report PDF](https://www.rif.org/sites/default/files/documents/2019/10/24/Support_Materials/BeeLineWestEdStudyFinal.pdf)
  - Computers in Human Behavior Reports (May 2022) [peer-reviewed]: 2nd–3rd graders (no ADHD): visual anchors "may be useful for some beginning readers in some situations but can also impede readability"; 2nd graders read slower with BeeLine on level-appropriate texts; comprehension may suffer for 3rd graders. — [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/b48617b6416f4030ae9bf57ab79d1318)

- Bionic Reading (as implemented in Readwise Reader): Readwise's June 2022 public test, 2,074 testers, paired design: 325.3 wpm (Bionic) vs 327.9 wpm (plain), i.e. 2.6 wpm slower with Bionic, <1%, "no evidence it improves reading speed"; earlier ~30-per-group pilot showed +14 wpm, one-tailed p = 0.14 (n.s.); Quartz coverage of the pilot reported ~4% speed gain and a comprehension decrease. ### Inferences
- Commercial "ADHD reading" claims rest on dyslexia research (font/spacing), general-population engagement data, or testimonials. The highest-quality tool evaluations (WestEd n = 530; Readwise n = 2,074) are not peer-reviewed and show small or null effects; the one peer-reviewed BeeLine study shows possible harm to readability for young readers.
- Any new tool should avoid citing these products as evidence of efficacy for ADHD and should plan its own evaluation, ideally with a comparison condition, since even the academic ADHD interface studies (Lee et al. 2026; Zhu et al. 2025) lack controls.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed evaluation of Helperbird, Speechify or Readwise with any population was found.
- The BDA/Knowl Hill Immersive Reader report itself was not located (only secondary coverage).
- A systematic review of reading-support tools specifically for ADHD was not found; the search budget ran out before a PubMed/Scholar-style query on "text-to-speech ADHD adults randomized" could be refined.
