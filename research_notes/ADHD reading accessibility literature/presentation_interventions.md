# Text presentation and typography interventions for reading with ADHD (and related populations)

Scope note: these notes cover text-presentation manipulations (segmentation, typography, fixation bolding, highlighting, TTS/bimodal, reading guides, pacing, dark mode / clutter removal, simplification/summarization, personalisation). For each study the population tested is stated explicitly. Verification status: all citations below were located via web search in October 2026; where only secondary summaries (press, blogs, aggregator pages) were reachable rather than the primary paper, that is stated. Direct full-text fetches from publishers (ACM DL, Springer, PMC, Readwise) were blocked by the egress proxy in this session, so sample sizes/effects come from abstracts, repository pages and secondary summaries, as flagged. Items I could not verify are listed under Gaps rather than cited.

Method caveat for the report writer: the single most important finding across all questions is that **ADHD-specific controlled evidence on text presentation is extremely thin**. Only a handful of studies tested ADHD readers directly (one 2026 ACM eye-tracking/pupillometry font study; one 2019 print-vs-digital comprehension study; one 2025/26 TTS eye-tracking study; one 2017 Swedish TTS study moderated by ADHD symptoms; one n=3 single-case highlighting study; 1990s–2000 "added colour" studies from Zentall's lab). Almost everything else is dyslexia or general-population evidence.

## Key question 1: Text segmentation / chunking, shorter paragraphs, headings, up-front summaries, bullet lists

### Takeaway
No controlled study testing chunking, paragraph length, bullet lists or up-front summaries specifically with ADHD readers was found; the only ADHD-specific signal is qualitative (interview data in a 2026 ACM study where ADHD participants said dense/lengthy text led them to avoid reading and chunking helped them sustain attention). For general adult readers there is solid experimental evidence that headings improve memory for topic structure and processing of topic sentences, but no pooled effect size was retrieved.

### Cited Findings
- ADHD-specific (qualitative only): a 2026 ACM in-lab study of readers with and without ADHD ("Reading with Diversity in Mind: Pupillometry and Typography Towards Inclusive Design for ADHD Readers", published 13 April 2026, DOI 10.1145/3772363.3799383) reports from follow-up interviews that ADHD participants preferred customizable, distraction-free reading interfaces; a search summary of the same paper states that dense or lengthy text led participants to avoid reading and that splitting text into chunks helped them sustain attention. This is interview/preference data, not a controlled test of chunking. Sample size was not retrievable from the abstract. — [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/full/10.1145/3772363.3799383); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3772363.3799383)
- General adult readers, headings: Lorch, Lemarié & Chen (2013), "Signaling topic structure via headings or preview sentences", found that college students' memory for subtopics was better for text with headings; headings and preview sentences did not differ on memory for simple facts; outlining improved when texts had topic-structure signals. — [University of Kentucky Scholars record](https://scholars.uky.edu/en/publications/signaling-topic-structure-via-headings-or-preview-sentences/)
- General adult readers, headings (eye tracking): Hyönä & Lorch (2004), "Effects of topic headings on text processing: evidence from adult readers' eye fixation patterns", found that the presence of topic headings facilitated processing of topic sentences and increased the number of topics mentioned in written summaries. — [University of Turku research record](https://research.utu.fi/converis/portal/detail/Publication/1833920?lang=en_US)
- Theory of signalling devices (headings etc.): Lemarié, Lorch & Péry-Woodley's SARA framework (Discours, 2012) describes headings as influencing comprehension by activating prior knowledge. — [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/b268e54e7dcb49e7b7daa223464636d6)
- General readers, line-at-a-time / line-focus presentation (closest controlled analogue to "chunking"): see Key question 5 (Digital Reading Rulers, CHI 2023) — rulers that isolate a band of lines improved reading speed for readers with dyslexia; participants' most cited reasons were "keep my place" and "focus". — [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3544548.3581367)
- Industry/marketing (no controlled data): vendor pages for ADHD reading apps (e.g., Nook, Helperbird) recommend "one paragraph or one line at a time" chunking as a strategy, without study data. — [getnook.net blog](https://getnook.net/blog/how-to-read-with-adhd); [Helperbird blog](https://www.helperbird.com/blog/best-free-study-tools-for-students-with-adhd/)

### Inferences
- The general-population heading literature (Lorch lab) supports headings as a low-risk default for improving structural memory, but there is no evidence it has been tested in ADHD samples; any ADHD benefit is extrapolated.
- The ADHD interview data (2026 ACM study) and the reading-ruler data (dyslexia, with co-occurring ADHD allowed) point in the same direction — presenting less text at once helps sustained attention/place-keeping — but this is preference and dyslexia-speed evidence, not ADHD comprehension evidence.

### Gaps
- No controlled study on paragraph length, bullet lists, or up-front summaries with ADHD participants was found in any search.
- No meta-analysis of headings/signalling effects with a pooled effect size was retrieved (a Lorch meta-analysis could not be located; searches returned only individual studies).
- No search returned any study on "progressive disclosure" of text for ADHD.

## Key question 2: Typography — fonts (OpenDyslexic, Dyslexie, serif vs sans), size, letter spacing, line spacing, line length, contrast, colour overlays; did any test ADHD readers?

### Takeaway
Only one study (the 2026 ACM pupillometry paper) tested fonts/sizes with ADHD readers, and its retrievable results concern group differences (ADHD readers read faster with fewer saccades) and a speed–comprehension trade-off rather than a "best font for ADHD". For dyslexia the evidence is consistent: specialist fonts (OpenDyslexic, Dyslexie) do not help beyond their spacing; larger letter spacing and larger font size speed reading (Zorzi 2012; Rello & Baeza-Yates 2017; Marinus 2016); sans-serif/roman/monospaced beat serif/italic (Rello & Baeza-Yates 2013); warm backgrounds beat cool (Rello & Bigham 2017); colour overlays have weak/unreliable effects (Henderson et al. 2013); positive polarity (dark-on-light) beats dark mode for proofreading in both young and older adults (Piepenbrock et al. 2013).

### Cited Findings

Sub-heading: ADHD readers
- "Reading with Diversity in Mind" (ACM, 2026, DOI 10.1145/3772363.3799383): in-lab readability study with eye tracking and pupillometry comparing readers with and without ADHD; passages shown in four Roboto variants (Default, Monospace, Serif, Slab) at 14, 16 or 18 px. Reported results: ADHD status had a large effect on reading speed in mixed linear models; in Poisson regression ADHD participants read faster and made fewer saccades (authors link this to broader visual scanning); significant correlations between reading speed and mean pupil dilation; the authors describe first findings as pointing to a trade-off between reading speed and comprehension; interviews showed preferences for customizable, distraction-free interfaces. Authors claim to be the first to examine ADHD, fonts and pupillometry together. Sample size and per-font comprehension figures were not retrievable. — [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/full/10.1145/3772363.3799383)
- ADHD-related eye movements (context): a 2022 Annals of Dyslexia eye-tracking study of 96 children found the oculomotor pattern of children with ADHD without comorbid dyslexia was similar to typically developing children (DOI 10.1007/s11881-022-00269-x). — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11881-022-00269-x). Replicated by Donbaloğlu et al. (2025, Reading Research Quarterly, DOI 10.1002/rrq.70047): ADHD group eye movements did not differ from controls while the dyslexia group differed from both. — [Wiley](https://ila.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/rrq.70047)
- Small observational ADHD font study (low quality): a ResearchGate-hosted 2023 paper, "Identification of Digital Font Size and Font Type to Enhance the Attention Span of Children Living with ADHD in a Typical Learning Environment", reports the Thai handwriting-style "Mali" font held children's attention longest; it is small and observational and the full method was not retrievable. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/369753521_Identification_of_Digital_Font_Size_and_Font_Type_to_Enhance_the_Attention_Span_of_Children_Living_with_ADHD_in_a_Typical_Learning_Environment)
- Added colour for attention-deficit students (Zentall lab, secondary source): the NCEO Accommodations Toolkit summarises Zentall et al. (2000) as finding that elementary students with attention-related disabilities performed moderately better on a reading test with colour backgrounds and black font than with a black-and-white format; Lee & Zentall (2002) found maths performance of elementary/middle-school students with ADHD improved in a high-stimulation vs low-stimulation testing condition. Primary texts not retrieved. — [NCEO Accommodations Toolkit: colour/contrast research](https://publications.ici.umn.edu/nceo/accommodations-toolkit/color-contrast-research)

Sub-heading: Dyslexia-specific fonts (OpenDyslexic, Dyslexie)
- Rello & Baeza-Yates (2013), "Good Fonts for Dyslexia", ASSETS 2013 (DOI 10.1145/2513383.2513447): eye-tracking study, 48 participants with dyslexia, 12 fonts. Sans serif, monospaced and roman styles significantly improved reading performance over serif, proportional and italic (effects stronger on fixation duration than reading time); italics consistently reduced readability; OpenDyslexic was neither better nor worse than standard fonts and participants significantly preferred Verdana and Helvetica over it; recommended fonts: Helvetica, Courier, Arial, Verdana, CMU. — [summary page](https://a11y-paradise.onrender.com/reviews/69d25a338a41f1818e3d54ca); [BYU editing-research summary](https://editingresearch.byu.edu/2020/05/28/which-fonts-are-best-for-dyslexia/)
- Rello & Baeza-Yates (2016), "The Effect of Font Type on Screen Readability by People with Dyslexia", ACM TACCESS: 97 native Spanish speakers, 48 with dyslexia, 49 without (a different design from the 2013 paper; take care not to conflate the two "48" figures). — [summary page](https://a11y-paradise.onrender.com/reviews/69cd7af2bbd7e71582e41407)
- Wery & Diliberto (2017; online 2016), "The effect of a specialized dyslexia font, OpenDyslexic, on reading rate and accuracy", Annals of Dyslexia 67(2):114–127, DOI 10.1007/s11881-016-0127-1: single-subject alternating-treatment design, elementary students with dyslexia, OpenDyslexic vs Arial vs Times New Roman on letter naming, word and nonsense-word reading; no improvement in rate or accuracy for individuals or group; no student preferred OpenDyslexic. (Participant count not shown in abstracts; the commonly cited n=12 could not be verified.) — [PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5629233/); [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11881-016-0127-1)
- Kuster, van Weerdenburg, Gompel & Bosman (2018), "Dyslexie font does not benefit reading in children with or without dyslexia", Annals of Dyslexia 68:25–42: Exp. 1 children with dyslexia read text in Dyslexie vs Arial — no faster/more accurate; Exp. 2 children with and without dyslexia read word lists in three fonts — no advantage; most children preferred Arial or Times New Roman and preference did not track performance; authors conclude the font "neither benefits nor impedes". — [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Dyslexie-font-does-not-benefit-reading-in-children-Kuster-Weerdenburg/ff4528589d7a038b4c3f0f297d8ae0826d053538); [d-nb.info PDF](https://d-nb.info/1151792292/34)
- Marinus et al. (2016), "A special font for people with dyslexia: Does it work and, if so, why?", Dyslexia: 39 low-progress readers learning to read in English; ~7% faster in Dyslexie than standard-spaced Arial, but when Arial's within-word and between-word spacing was matched to Dyslexie's the difference disappeared; conclusion: the benefit is due to spacing, not letter shapes. — [Macquarie University record](https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/a-special-font-for-people-with-dyslexia-does-it-work-and-if-so-wh/); [BrainFacts summary](https://www.brainfacts.org/diseases-and-disorders/childhood-disorders/2017/can-special-fonts-help-people-with-dyslexia-082117)
- Joseph & Powell (2022), "Does a specialist typeface affect how fluently children with and without dyslexia process letters, words and passages?", Dyslexia 28(4):448–470, DOI 10.1002/dys.1727 (open access): 71 children aged 8–12 (37 with dyslexia), Dyslexie vs Calibri with letter and word spacing held constant; no difference in word or passage reading for either group; small advantage for Dyslexie in rapid letter naming. — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/dys.1727); [University of Reading record](https://reading-clone.eprints-hosting.org/106411)
- Secondary reviews concur: Shanahan (Reading Rockets) summarises that most studies found no improvement in rate, accuracy or eye fixations with special fonts and that dyslexic readers preferred standard fonts; International Dyslexia Association likewise. — [Reading Rockets](https://www.readingrockets.org/blogs/shanahan-on-literacy/what-about-special-fonts-kids-dyslexia-or-other-reading-problems); [IDA](https://dyslexiaida.org/do-special-fonts-help-people-with-dyslexia/)
- Vendor claim (not independent): dyslexiefont.com's research page reports mostly positive findings; it is run by the typeface's makers. — [dyslexiefont.com](https://dyslexiefont.com/research)

Sub-heading: Letter spacing and font size
- Zorzi et al. (2012), "Extra-large letter spacing improves reading in dyslexia", PNAS 109(28):11455–11459, DOI 10.1073/pnas.1205566109: 74 dyslexic children (34 Italian, 40 French) aged 8–14 read 24 sentences in Times New Roman 14 pt with standard vs +2.5 pt spacing, two sessions two weeks apart, randomised order; extra spacing sped reading by >20% and doubled text-reading accuracy; younger reading-level-matched controls did not benefit; mechanism attributed to crowding. A critique (Skottun & Skoyles) and authors' reply appeared in PNAS 109(44):E2959; authors note substantial individual differences behind the group effect. — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3396504); [MedicalXpress](https://m.medicalxpress.com/news/2012-06-wider-letter-spacing-dyslexics.html); [reply, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3497756)
- Rello & Baeza-Yates (2017), "How to present more readable text for people with dyslexia", Universal Access in the Information Society 16(1):29–49: eye-tracking study, 92 participants (46 dyslexia, 46 controls); parameters: font size, colour, column width, character/line/paragraph spacing; larger text and larger character spacing led both groups to read significantly faster. An 18 pt "optimal size" is cited only in a secondary blog; the paper's exact size guidance was not verified. — [UPF repository](https://repositori.upf.edu/items/e0ca6f40-a0fa-490f-9d03-53d043270deb/full); [changedyslexia blog](https://blog.changedyslexia.org/?p=345)
- Rello et al. (2012), "Layout Guidelines for Web Text and a Web Service to Improve Accessibility for Dyslexics" (W4A 2012): 22 dyslexic participants; font size had a significant effect on readability and understandability while line spacing did not; 26 pt performed best. — [summary page](https://a11y-paradise.onrender.com/reviews/69c089c6b56623a3e201b1ee); [W3C text-customization note](https://w3.org/WAI/RD/2012/text-customization/r11)
- Masulli et al. (2018), Vision Research: 15 dyslexic and 30 non-dyslexic children; increasing font size and character spacing significantly reduced fixation duration and increased number/amplitude of prosaccades in all groups (i.e., benefit not dyslexia-specific). — [HAL record](https://hal.parisnanterre.fr/hal-03152723v1)
- Katzir, Hershko & Halamish (2013), "The Effect of Font Size on Reading Comprehension on Second and Fifth Grade Children: Bigger Is Not Always Better", PLOS ONE: smaller font lowered comprehension for second graders; fifth graders showed no penalty (decreasing size yielded higher comprehension). General-population children. — [PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3777945/)
- Bernard et al. (2002), "Comparison of Popular Online Fonts: Which Size and Type is Best?", Usability News 4.1 (Wichita State SURL); related 2001 study: 12-pt fonts read reliably faster than 10-pt; Arial and Times New Roman read faster than Courier, Schoolbook, Georgia; 2003 IJHCS follow-up: 10-pt anti-aliased Arial read slower than other conditions, 12-pt Arial preferred. General adult readers; secondary summaries only. — [HFI newsletter](https://www.humanfactors.com/Newsletters/more_about_fonts.html); [OSCQR citation](https://oscqr.suny.edu/standard23/)
- Line length: Dyson & Haselgrove (2001), "The influence of reading speed and line length on the effectiveness of reading from screen", IJHCS 54:585–612: compared 35/55/75/95 cpl; 95 cpl read fastest, with 55 cpl described (in secondary sources) as supporting effective speed and comprehension; Dyson & Kipping (1998) found 100-character lines read faster than 25-character lines with constant comprehension. General adult readers. — [Shaikh & Chaparro abstract (SAGE)](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/154193120304701112); [HFI summary](https://humanfactors.com/newsletters/optimal_line_length.html)

Sub-heading: Contrast polarity, background colour, overlays
- Piepenbrock, Mayr, Mund & Buchner (2013), "Positive display polarity is advantageous for both younger and older adults", Ergonomics 56(7): visual acuity and proofreading tasks in young (18–33) and older (60–85) adults; positive polarity (dark text on light) advantage for both groups; follow-up (Piepenbrock, Mayr & Buchner) found smaller pupils and better proofreading with positive polarity, consistent with a display-luminance explanation; participants did not perceive a readability difference. General population; no ADHD/dyslexia. — [Düsseldorf repository](https://docserv.uni-duesseldorf.de/servlets/DerivateServlet/Derivate-31413); [Mannheim record](https://madoc.bib.uni-mannheim.de/26118/); [NN/g summary via Slashdot](https://hardware.slashdot.org/story/20/02/16/2041240/dark-mode-vs-light-mode-which-is-better)
- Individual variation in polarity: a 2024 IEEE VIS short paper (arXiv 2409.10841) found the better-performing polarity varied by individual across age groups. — [arXiv](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2409.10841)
- Rello & Bigham (2017), "Good Background Colors for Readers: A Study of People with and without Dyslexia", ASSETS 2017 pp. 72–80: 341 participants (89 with dyslexia); reading time and mouse-travel as readability measures, comprehension as control; Peach, Orange and Yellow significantly improved reading over Blue, Blue Grey and Green for both groups; a secondary page reports mean reading time for dyslexic participants 14.85 s (Peach) vs 21.57 s (Blue Grey) and rank correlation between groups rho=0.964 (figures not verified against PDF). — [PDF (changedyslexia.org)](https://www.changedyslexia.org/publications/pdfs/2017-ASSETS-Good%20Background%20Colors.pdf); [summary page](https://a11y-paradise.onrender.com/reviews/69c699716bf81f6dadea1e07)
- Cream vs white follow-up: a 2026 Open University of Israel (Chais) poster found a consistent preference for cream backgrounds among participants with reading difficulties/dyslexia, with only one measured dimension reaching significance. — [OpenU poster PDF](https://www.openu.ac.il/Lists/MediaServer_Documents/innovation/chais/2026/b4_7.pdf)
- Coloured overlays: Henderson, Tsogka & Snowling (2013), "Questioning the benefits that coloured overlays can have for reading in students with and without dyslexia": within-subject design; overlays sped reading of jumbled text in both groups but the dyslexic group gained no more than controls; no improvement in rate or comprehension of connected text; speed gain unreliable and shrank at retest. — [Oxford Psychiatry record](https://www.psy.ox.ac.uk/publications/385403); [White Rose eprint](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/85990/). Earlier Wilkins work is cited (secondary) as ~25% speed gains with a self-chosen overlay. — [Frontiers 2014 review PDF](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00833/pdf). A registered randomised crossover trial of overlays/tinted lenses (NCT02680223, City, University of London) exists; results not found. — [ClinicalTrials.gov](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT02680223)
- "Sans Forgetica" (memory-boosting font claim): Taylor et al. (2020), Memory, found no memory benefit and sometimes impaired recall; the font felt harder to read without producing desirable difficulty; educational-passage comprehension also not improved. — [Warwick WRAP](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/136656); [BPS Research Digest](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/hard-read-font-was-designed-boost-memory-it-might-not-actually-work)
- Popular ADHD-font claims (no controlled evidence): blog posts recommending Lexend, Atkinson Hyperlegible or OpenDyslexic "for ADHD" cite no ADHD study; one such page itself states no peer-reviewed studies have tested fonts specifically for ADHD. — [getnook.net](https://getnook.net/blog/best-fonts-for-adhd-reading); [designyourway](https://www.designyourway.net/blog/best-fonts-for-adhd/); [alexandria.live](https://alexandria.live/use-cases/reading-fonts)

### Inferences
- For dyslexia, the manipulations with replicated speed benefits are generous letter spacing and larger font size, plus plain sans-serif/roman fonts; the specialist-font effect is explained by spacing. Since these effects also appear in non-dyslexic children (Masulli 2018) and adult controls (Rello & Baeza-Yates 2017), they are plausible general-reader defaults, but there is no ADHD-specific test.
- Eye-tracking data showing ADHD-without-dyslexia children read with typical oculomotor patterns suggest crowding-based manipulations (spacing) may matter less for ADHD than for dyslexia, whereas attention/engagement manipulations may matter more — consistent with the ADHD "read faster, fewer saccades, speed–comprehension trade-off" pattern reported in the 2026 ACM study.
- The Zentall-lab colour findings (moderate benefit from added colour for attention-deficit students) are the only ADHD-relevant contrast/colour results; they are old, secondary-sourced and about test formats, not continuous reading.

### Gaps
- Sample size, per-condition comprehension results and proceedings venue of the 2026 ACM ADHD pupillometry paper were not retrievable (ACM DL blocked).
- No study of letter spacing, line spacing or line length with ADHD participants was found.
- Primary texts of Zentall et al. (2000) and Lee & Zentall (2002) were not retrieved; only the NCEO toolkit summary.
- Dobres et al. (2017, Applied Ergonomics) on glance legibility and polarity could not be verified in this session.
- No evidence for Lexend was found in any search result (neither supporting nor refuting).

## Key question 3: Bionic Reading and "fixation bolding" methods

### Takeaway
Every controlled test located — an industry sample of 2,074 (Readwise 2022), a lab study of 32 students (Snell 2024), a pre-registered eye-tracking study of 90 skilled adults (Parker et al. 2026), and a print/screen eye-tracking study (Možina et al. 2025) — finds no gain in reading speed or comprehension from bolding word beginnings; eye-tracking shows the bolding shifts initial landing position on long words and in one study lengthened reading time. None tested ADHD readers.

### Cited Findings
- Industry (blog, not peer reviewed): Readwise (2022), "Does Bionic Reading actually work? We timed over 2,000 readers": 2,074 testers read two 1,000-word essays split in halves, posted June 2022 to Hacker News/Reddit/Twitter; readers were 2.6 wpm slower with Bionic Reading (difference <1%); comprehension virtually identical; authors found no evidence of any effect on speed or comprehension. — [Readwise blog](https://blog.readwise.io/bionic-reading-results/). Earlier Readwise pilot (61 participants) reported a non-significant ~4% speed gain with a comprehension decrease, as covered by Quartz. — [Quartz](https://qz.com/2182666/does-bionic-reading-work-typographers-and-scientists-weigh-in); [Readwise pilot post](https://blog.readwise.io/does-bionic-reading-actually-work/)
- Peer reviewed: Snell (2024), "No, Bionic Reading does not work", Acta Psychologica 247:104304, DOI 10.1016/j.actpsy.2024.104304: 32 university students (no prior Bionic use) read 100 paragraphs, half Bionic, half normal; no significant difference in reading times; no advantage for fast or slow readers. — [VU Amsterdam record](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/no-bionic-reading-does-not-work/); [SciSpace](https://scispace.com/papers/no-bionic-reading-does-not-work-2jnr1mxn06)
- Peer reviewed, registered report: Parker, Lee, Wang & Joseph (2026), "Does the selective bolding of characters within words improve reading performance in skilled adult readers? Evidence from eye movements", Royal Society Open Science 13(9), DOI 10.1098/rsos.250177 (published 2 Sept 2026; Stage 1 protocol OSF 10.17605/OSF.IO/ND397): 90 skilled adult readers, eye movements recorded while reading single sentences in three typefaces; bolding pulled the first fixation closer to the start of 8-letter words more than 4-letter words; no change in reading speed, word skipping or comprehension. — [DOI](https://dx.doi.org/10.1098/rsos.250177); [Phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-09-bionic-faster-scientists.html); [ScienceBlog](https://scienceblog.com/s-bionic-reading-bolds-the-first-half-of-each-word-on-the-promise-of-more-efficient-reading-and-a-registered-eye-tracking-study-of-90-skilled-adult-readers-found-the-bolding-moved-their-first/)
- Peer reviewed: Možina, Kovačević & Blaznik (2025), "Usability of Bionic Reading on Different Mediums: Eye-Tracking Study", SAGE Open, DOI 10.1177/21582440251376158: students and middle-aged readers; Georgia and Verdana; screen and print; Bionic formatting lengthened reading time; no advantage on paper; comprehension effects described as unclear. Sample size not retrieved. — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/21582440251376158)
- Peer reviewed (journal not confirmed): "Guiding the Gaze: How Bionic Reading Influences Eye Movements" (PMC12565662, 2025): reports Bionic font does not significantly change eye movements during reading. — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12565662/)
- Cited within Parker et al. (secondary via ScienceBlog/Phys.org): Spear et al. (2025) found start-bolded paragraphs produced shorter but more numerous fixations with no change in per-word reading time; Beelders (2025) reported a null effect of bolding the first few characters. Phys.org describes a 2025 Attention, Perception & Psychophysics study finding that bolding word beginnings hindered rather than helped — I could not confirm the exact citation. — [Phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-09-bionic-faster-scientists.html)
- Method note: default Bionic rule bolds half the letters of each word (rounding up for odd lengths). — [ScienceBlog](https://scienceblog.com/?p=600457)

### Inferences
- Across industry and peer-reviewed tests (total >2,200 readers), the direction of effect is null-to-slightly-negative for speed and null for comprehension; the only robust effect is oculomotor (landing position), which does not translate into efficiency.
- No study tested ADHD or dyslexic readers, so the popular claim that Bionic Reading "helps ADHD" rests on no controlled evidence; the general-reader nulls make a large ADHD-specific benefit unlikely but untested.

### Gaps
- No "Hayes" preprint on Bionic Reading was found in any search; I cannot confirm such a preprint exists.
- No Bionic Reading study with ADHD or dyslexia participants was found.
- Sample sizes for Možina et al. (2025) and "Guiding the Gaze" (2025) were not retrieved.

## Key question 4: Highlighting key terms / keyword emphasis, text-to-speech / bimodal reading, reading guides (line focus), progressive disclosure, adjustable pacing (RSVP)

### Takeaway
TTS has the strongest ADHD-specific evidence of any presentation aid: a 2025/26 eye-tracking study found TTS improved comprehension for junior-high students with ADHD-related reading difficulties (but not dyslexia), and a 2017 Swedish randomised cross-over (N=49) found TTS raised reading rate regardless of ADHD symptoms with age-dependent comprehension effects. Keyword bolding improved comprehension for dyslexic adults (n=62) and reduced off-task behaviour in three ADHD children; self-highlighting helped ADHD university students only on paper. Reading rulers sped dyslexic readers; RSVP (Spritz) impaired comprehension above normal reading speed in general readers.

### Cited Findings

Sub-heading: Keyword emphasis / highlighting
- Dyslexia: Rello, Saggion & Baeza-Yates (2014), "Keyword Highlighting Improves Comprehension for People with Dyslexia", PITR workshop @ EACL 2014, pp. 30–37, DOI 10.3115/v1/W14-1204: eye-tracking study with 62 participants (31 with dyslexia); bolding the text's main ideas improved comprehension for participants with dyslexia (described by the authors as the first such result for this group). — [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/W14-1204); [PDF](https://aclanthology.org/W14-1204.pdf)
- ADHD (single-case): Santos & Albuquerque (2021), "Effect of Words Highlighting in School Tasks upon Typical ADHD Behaviors", Psicologia: Teoria e Pesquisa 37:e37302, DOI 10.1590/0102.3772e37302: three elementary students with ADHD, multiple-baseline plus reversal design; highlighted-word conditions produced fewer instances of inattention and hyperactivity/impulsivity and more correct answers on school tasks. — [periodicos.unb.br](https://periodicos.unb.br/index.php/revistaptp/article/view/22201/34195)
- ADHD (self-highlighting; medium effect): "Pay attention to digital text: The impact of the media on text comprehension and self-monitoring in higher-education students with ADHD" (2019), Research in Developmental Disabilities: 45 students with ADHD and 61 matched controls read an expository text digitally or in print; in the digital condition ADHD students had significantly lower comprehension and were overconfident in predicted success; ADHD group poorly regulated study time only for digital text; in print, ADHD students spent more time reading but matched controls on comprehension and calibration; when instructed to highlight, performance improved only in the printed condition. Authors conclude digitally displayed text is more challenging for students with ADHD, particularly when the task favours self-regulated learning skills. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0891422219300605); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/332510916_Pay_attention_to_digital_text_The_impact_of_the_media_on_text_comprehension_and_self-monitoring_in_higher-education_students_with_ADHD)
- General adolescents: a 2024 Learning and Individual Differences-type study ("Ink and pixels") of 191 seventh graders found digital reading reduced cognitive load without affecting comprehension; highlighting interacted with reading self-efficacy. Not ADHD-specific. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1041608024001973)
- Vendor claim: "bold-letter reading significantly improves comprehension" for ADHD is asserted without citation. — [goblinxadhd blog](https://www.goblinxadhd.com/blog/unlocking-clarity-mastering-adhd-bold-letter-read/)

Sub-heading: Text-to-speech / bimodal reading
- ADHD vs dyslexia (eye tracking): Chen, Hung & Jian (accepted 2025, in press 2026), "The effects of text-to-speech on reading comprehension in junior high school students with reading disabilities: an eye-tracking study in Taiwan", Reading and Writing, DOI 10.1007/s11145-025-10738-5: TTS significantly improved comprehension for students with ADHD-related reading difficulties but not for students with dyslexia; for dyslexic students TTS reduced reading time and mental effort while keeping comprehension near silent-reading levels; typically developing students gained nothing and their reading patterns were disrupted; ADHD students showed longer fixation durations under TTS (interpreted as increased focus). The related 2024 NTNU master's thesis reports 60 students from 14 junior high schools; the journal sample was not verified. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11145-025-10738-5); [NTNU record](https://scholar.lib.ntnu.edu.tw/en/publications/the-effects-of-text-to-speech-on-reading-comprehension-in-junior-/); [NTNU thesis](https://etds.lib.ntnu.edu.tw/thesis/detail/db514e470a528085f4fa0365f5e410de)
- Reading disability with ADHD-symptom moderation: "The Compensatory Effect of Text-to-Speech Technology on Reading Comprehension and Reading Rate in Swedish Schoolchildren With Reading Disability: The Moderating Effect of Inattention and Hyperactivity Symptoms Differs by Grade Groups" (2017), DOI 10.1177/0162643417742898 (SAGE; ISSN prefix 0162-6434 corresponds to Journal of Special Education Technology — journal name not confirmed in search): N=49 randomised cross-over (TTS vs self-reading); TTS raised reading rate in both grade groups independent of SDQ ADHD score; TTS improved comprehension significantly in the younger group but not the older group; higher SDQ ADHD score was associated with less comprehension gain in the younger group and greater gain in the older group. — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0162643417742898); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/321364041_The_Compensatory_Effect_of_Text-to-Speech_Technology_on_Reading_Comprehension_and_Reading_Rate_in_Swedish_Schoolchildren_With_Reading_Disability_The_Moderating_Effect_of_Inattention_and_Hyperactivity_)
- ADHD adults (qualitative): "ADHD Adults' Experiences in Digital Reading Environments" (edtecharchives journal) is a phenomenological study of AI narration; no effect sizes. — [edtecharchives](https://edtecharchives.org/journal/2222/20815)
- ADHD students in higher education (qualitative): "Reading by Listening in Academia: Students with ADHD's Use of Audiobooks and Text-to-Speech in Higher Education" (Lund University record). — [LUP](https://lup.lub.lu.se/record/67f3db85-dfb1-48af-ba43-a7c32331d253)
- Low quality: an Indonesian single-subject e-learning study of TTS for ADHD students reports qualitative progress only. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/369108630_The_Role_of_Text_To_Speech_Assistive_Technology_to_Improve_Reading_Ability_in_E-Learning_for_ADHD_Students)
- Vendor claims of "25–30% comprehension gains" from TTS for ADHD cite no source. — [text2speech.co blog](https://text2speech.co/blog/2026/05/text-to-speech-for-adhd/)
- Simplification delivered by audio (general readers): Barai, Leroy & Ahmed (in press, Communications of the AIS) replicated Leroy's simplification effect with 44 participants listening to audio of original vs simplified texts. — [AIS eLibrary](https://aisel.aisnet.org/cais/vol57/iss1/48)

Sub-heading: Reading guides / line focus
- Niklaus, Cai, Bylinskii & Wallace (2023), "Digital Reading Rulers: Evaluating Inclusively Designed Rulers for Readers With Dyslexia and Without", CHI 2023, DOI 10.1145/3544548.3581367: 177 crowdsourced participants (91 with dyslexia, 86 without; co-occurring ADHD permitted); four rulers (Grey Bar, Lightbox, Shade, Underline); Grey Bar, Shade and Underline improved reading speed for dyslexic participants by ~19, 16 and 12 wpm vs no ruler; no single ruler preferred by all; dyslexic readers tended to favour Lightbox; top reasons: "keep my place" and "focus"; comprehension effect reported in secondary summaries but not verified. A JOV abstract from the same group reports an average 86 wpm gain for a participant's fastest ruler vs none — a different analysis, do not mix with CHI figures. — [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3544548.3581367); [Readability Matters highlight](https://readabilitymatters.org/articles/research-highlight-digital-reading-rulers); [JOV abstract](https://jov.arvojournals.org/article.aspx?articleid=2777804)
- BeeLine Reader (colour-gradient line guidance): a 2022 Leiden University study of beginning readers ran two experiments (headline result not retrievable); vendor-associated pilots (CNET) and company statements claim benefit "particularly for ADD and dyslexia" while acknowledging independent study is still needed. — [Leiden repository](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/handle/1887/3448262); [Behavioral Scientist](https://behavioralscientist.org/speed-reading-apps-open-research-questions/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BeeLine_Reader)

Sub-heading: Adjustable pacing / RSVP
- Benedetto et al. (2015), "Rapid serial visual presentation in reading: The case of Spritz", Computers in Human Behavior 45:352–358: general readers read a book excerpt via Spritz or traditionally; Spritz impaired literal comprehension and increased visual fatigue, attributed to suppressed parafoveal processing and regressions and reduced blinking. — [abstract mirror](https://www.tsw.it/en/laboratories-of-experiences/scientific-publications/page/5/); [datalearner listing](https://datalearner.com:8443/academic/journal-papers/0747-5632/volumes-and-issues/155/paper-detail/35002)
- Ricciardi & Di Nocera (2017), "Not so fast: A reply to Benedetto et al. (2015)", CHB 69:381–385: two studies, 172 participants; comprehension loss occurred when presentation speed exceeded normal reading speed (>250 wpm), attributed to encoding difficulty rather than attention/workload. — [datalearner listing](https://www.datalearner.com/academic/journal-papers/0747-5632/volumes-and-issues/181/paper-detail/36580)
- Commentary: APS notes that removing the ability to re-read diminishes understanding. — [Psychological Science](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/?p=96800)
- Unevaluated tool: arXiv 2307.14544 describes an AI "speed reading tool for students with ADHD, dyslexia and short attention span"; the search did not surface a user study. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.14544)

### Inferences
- TTS/bimodal reading is the only presentation aid with two independent studies showing ADHD-relevant comprehension benefits; the mechanism appears to be attentional (longer fixations, better engagement) rather than decoding, which is why dyslexic readers gain effort reduction but not comprehension.
- Keyword emphasis has converging small-sample evidence (dyslexic adults; 3 ADHD children) that it supports comprehension/on-task behaviour; it is a plausible ADHD candidate but lacks a group-level ADHD trial.
- The 2019 print-vs-digital ADHD study implies that any digital presentation intervention for ADHD must compensate for weaker self-regulation on screen; features that externalise regulation (rulers, pacing control, chunking) are theoretically aligned but untested in ADHD.
- RSVP removes regressions and re-reading; given ADHD readers' speed–comprehension trade-off, fixed-pace RSVP is a poor fit unless the user controls pace below ~250 wpm.

### Gaps
- No RSVP/Spritz study with ADHD participants was found.
- No controlled study of BeeLine Reader with ADHD or dyslexia was found; the Leiden 2022 results could not be read.
- Author list of the 2019 "Pay attention to digital text" paper was not captured by search results; verify before citing by name.
- Whether the Digital Reading Rulers paper reported ADHD-subgroup results could not be checked (full text blocked).
- Comprehension effect sizes for rulers not verified.

## Key question 5: Dark mode, reduced clutter / reader mode, animation and distraction removal

### Takeaway
No study tested reader mode / clutter removal with ADHD readers. General-population evidence shows positive polarity (light mode) outperforms dark mode on proofreading/acuity for young and older adults; animated and abrupt-onset ads reliably capture gaze and slow reading, with comprehension costs found in children (stronger with low inhibitory control) but inconsistently in adults.

### Cited Findings
- Dark vs light mode: Piepenbrock et al. (2013, Ergonomics 56(7)) — positive polarity better for visual acuity and proofreading in 18–33 and 60–85 year olds; advantage grows with smaller fonts (Human Factors follow-up per NN/g); no perceived difference by participants. — [Düsseldorf repository](https://docserv.uni-duesseldorf.de/servlets/DerivateServlet/Derivate-31413); [NN/g summary via Slashdot](https://hardware.slashdot.org/story/20/02/16/2041240/dark-mode-vs-light-mode-which-is-better)
- Dark background shades on mobile: a Croatian study (hrcak 493249) measured reading time and errors across dark background shades and found background colour significantly affected reading ability. — [hrcak](https://hrcak.srce.hr/clanak/493249)
- Ads and reading (adults): Simola et al. (2011), "The Impact of Salient Advertisements on Reading and Attention on Web Pages", JEP: Applied, DOI 10.1037/a0024042: ads on real pages drew overt attention during reading; longest looks when one ad animated and the other static; abrupt-onset ads pulled gaze, especially the ad right of the text; conclusion: salient ads disrupt reading. — [University of Turku record](https://research.utu.fi/converis/portal/detail/Publication/3550924?lang=en_US)
- Ads and comprehension (adults, eye tracking): Lan (Macquarie University thesis), "Reading online text content in the presence of visual distraction": four conditions from plain text to animated skyscraper; animated (and sometimes flashing-text) conditions produced more and longer fixations, strongest early in reading; comprehension did not differ significantly across conditions. — [Macquarie figshare](https://figshare.mq.edu.au/articles/thesis/Reading_online_text_content_in_the_presence_of_visual_distraction_Eye_movements_and_comprehension/24331030)
- Ad proximity: Frontiers in Psychology (2014, DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00211): ads closest to text slowed processing; intermediate and far distances did not differ; animation effect varied by task. — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00211/full)
- Children: a Lund University dissertation found animated ads reduced nine-year-olds' reading comprehension, more strongly in children with lower inhibitory control (the attention-relevant result). — [Lund University record](https://www.lunduniversity.lu.se/lup/publication/f8c12896-b789-4394-b9cd-1cece113fb08)
- Animated banners (TOCHI, Burke et al.): animated banners slowed common web tasks but were no more memorable than static ads. — [TOCHI PDF](https://thoughtcrumbs.com/publications/ToCHI04.pdf)
- ADHD preference data: interviews in the 2026 ACM pupillometry study found ADHD participants preferred customizable, distraction-free reading interfaces. — [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/full/10.1145/3772363.3799383)
- ADHD print vs digital: the 2019 RIDD study (45 ADHD, 61 controls) found digital presentation specifically disadvantaged ADHD students' comprehension and calibration relative to print. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0891422219300605)

### Inferences
- Removing animated/abrupt-onset peripheral content is supported by general eye-tracking evidence and by the child inhibitory-control finding, which is the closest proxy for ADHD; reader-mode benefit for ADHD is plausible but inferred.
- Dark mode has no performance evidence in its favour for general readers; the ADHD-blog claim that dark mode improves focus has no controlled support. Because polarity preference varies individually (2024 VIS paper), offering a toggle rather than defaulting to dark is the evidence-aligned design.

### Gaps
- No controlled study of "reader mode"/clutter removal with ADHD (or dyslexia) participants was found.
- No ADHD study of dark mode was found.
- Dobres et al. (2017) and Buchner & Baumgartner polarity papers could not be verified this session.

## Key question 6: Text simplification and automatic summarization as cognitive-accessibility aids

### Takeaway
User studies show lexical/sentence simplification improves comprehension for health-information consumers (Leroy 2013: 52%→63% correct, n=99), autistic adolescents/adults (Cerga-Pashoja 2019: 11.2 vs 10.0 correct, n=243), general readers of expert texts (Google 2025: +3.9 pp, n=4,563), and dyslexic readers (Rello 2013: frequent words → faster; shorter words → better understanding, n=46); people with Down syndrome/intellectual disability show weaker or null effects. **No simplification or summarization user study with ADHD participants was found.**

### Cited Findings
- Dyslexia: Rello, Baeza-Yates, Dempere-Marco & Saggion (2013), "Frequent Words Improve Readability and Short Words Improve Understandability for People with Dyslexia", INTERACT 2013 (LNCS 8120): eye tracking, 46 participants (23 dyslexia, 23 controls); swapping in more frequent words made dyslexic participants read significantly faster; shorter words improved their comprehension; controls showed no significant effects. — [HAL/IFIP](https://dl.ifip.org/IFIP-LNCS-8120/hal-01510517)
- Dyslexia (strategy): Rello, Baeza-Yates, Bott & Saggion (2013), "Simplify or help? Text simplification strategies for people with dyslexia", W4A 2013, DOI 10.1145/2461121.2461126: compared automatic replacement (SubsBest, LexSiS) with showing simpler synonyms on demand; the companion DysWebxia 2.0 paper (47 participants across laptop/tablet/phone) reports readers rated text more readable and comprehensible when simpler synonyms were available on demand. Numerical results of the W4A paper itself were not retrieved. — [summary page](https://a11y-paradise.onrender.com/reviews/69c080e9fbe1707d6a0f3216); [DysWebxia 2.0 PDF](https://www.changedyslexia.org/publications/pdfs/2013-W4A-DysWebxia%20More%20Accessible%20Text.pdf)
- Health consumers: Leroy, Endicott, Kauchak, Mouradi & Just (2013), "User evaluation of the effects of a text simplification algorithm using term familiarity on perception, understanding, learning, and information retention", JMIR 15(7):e144: 99 MTurk participants; perceived difficulty 3.2→2.3 (5-point, 1 easiest); comprehension 52%→63% correct (P<.001); learning +18% correct. The 2012 AMIA version (84 participants) found the perceived-difficulty effect but a non-significant comprehension gain (p=.097). — [JMIR](https://www.jmir.org/2013/7/e144); [AMIA/PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3540563)
- Autism: Cerga-Pashoja, Gaete, Shishkova & Jordanova (2019), "Improving Reading in Adolescents and Adults With High-Functioning Autism Through an Assistive Technology Tool: A Cross-Over Multinational Study", Frontiers in Psychiatry 10:546, DOI 10.3389/fpsyt.2019.00546: 243 participants (153 adults, 90 adolescents) in UK, Spain, Bulgaria; Open Book-simplified texts yielded more correct answers (M=11.2 vs 10.0, p<0.001), consistent across age groups and countries; blinded raters judged simplified texts easier (7.6 vs 8.7, p<0.001). — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2019.00546/epub); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6692437)
- Intellectual disability: Säuberli et al. (2024), "Digital Comprehensibility Assessment of Simplified Texts among Persons with Intellectual Disabilities", CHI 2024, DOI 10.1145/3613904.3642570: participants with and without ID read unsimplified, automatically and manually simplified German texts; measures (MCQ, perceived difficulty, response time, reading speed) varied significantly by reader group and by automatic vs manual simplification; comprehension questions were the most reliable measure. Cites Saggion et al. (2015), where persons with Down syndrome showed no significant comprehensibility difference between text versions. — [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3613904.3642570); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.13094v1); [UZH news](https://www.cl.uzh.ch/en/research-groups/accessibility/news/chi-paper-assessing-comprehensibility.html)
- Intellectual disability (2025): "Including people in the automatic text adaptation process: empirical findings from students with intellectual disability", Disability and Rehabilitation: Assistive Technology (DOI 10.1080/17483107.2025.2536701) — content not retrieved. — [Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17483107.2025.2536701)
- Cognitive impairment interface design: the Easier system user study (PMC10036960) found people with cognitive impairments could use simplification interfaces with satisfactory experience (usability, not comprehension outcomes). — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10036960/)
- Evaluation validity: Štajner et al. (2016), "Evaluating the Readability of Text Simplification Output for Readers with Cognitive Disabilities", LREC 2016: automatic readability metrics correlate poorly with human judgments; Simple Wikipedia is significantly more complex than EasyRead and LocalNews corpora. — [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/L16-1045/)
- General readers, LLM simplification (preprint, Google): "LLM-based Text Simplification and its Effect on User Comprehension and Cognitive Load" (arXiv 2505.01980, May 2025): randomised study, 4,563 participants, 31 texts in six domains; simplified-text readers answered ~3.9 pp more MCQs correctly (p<0.05), largest in PubMed (+14.6%), finance +5.5%, aerospace/CS +3.8%, law +3.5%; gain persisted without look-back; self-reported ease (simplified NASA-TLX) +0.33 on 5-point scale (p<0.05). Not peer reviewed at time of search. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.01980); [Google Research](https://research.google/pubs/llm-based-lossless-text-simplification-and-its-effect-on-user-comprehension-and-cognitive-load/)
- Possibly relevant to ADHD lexical simplification: "Effects of word length and word frequency among dyslexic, ADHD-I and typical readers" (PMC10063363) exists but its results were not retrieved. — [PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10063363/)

### Inferences
- Simplification effects are consistent in direction across health consumers, autistic readers, dyslexic readers and general readers (roughly +4 to +11 percentage points on comprehension questions), and weakest for intellectual disability; for ADHD the mechanism (reduced working-memory load) is plausible but entirely untested.
- The dyslexia literature favours on-demand synonyms over silent automatic replacement (user control and trust), which also fits the ADHD interview preference for customisable interfaces.

### Gaps
- No user study of text simplification or automatic summarization with ADHD participants was found.
- Alonzo et al. (Huenerfauth lab; automatic simplification for DHH adults) was not retrieved in this session; cannot report figures.
- Numerical results of Rello et al. "Simplify or help?" (W4A 2013) not retrieved.
- No study of up-front summaries/TL;DR for cognitive accessibility was found.

## Key question 7: Personalised / adaptive reading interfaces that let readers set parameters and measured outcomes

### Takeaway
The Adobe/Brown readability programme shows that individually chosen or recommended fonts change reading speed substantially (35% between fastest and slowest font per person; ~75 wpm; FontMART recommendations +14–25 wpm) without hurting comprehension, but readers' own preferences predict their fastest font only ~20% of the time; the Digital Reading Rulers study likewise found no universally best ruler. None of these studies targeted ADHD, and no adaptive interface study with ADHD outcomes was found.

### Cited Findings
- Wallace, Bylinskii, Dobres, Kerr, Berlow, Treitman, Kumawat, Arpin, Miller, Huang & Sawyer (2022), "Towards Individuated Reading Experiences: Different Fonts Increase Reading Speed for Different Individuals", ACM TOCHI (published 31 March 2022): 386 crowdsourced skilled adult readers aged 18–71 (per Adobe); reading speed differed by 35% between a person's fastest and slowest font, with no comprehension change; individual differences of ~75 wpm; materials and tools released. — [Adobe Research publication page](https://research.adobe.com/publication/towards-individuated-reading-experiences-different-fonts-increase-reading-speed-for-different-individuals); [TOCHI PDF](https://jeffhuang.com/papers/Readability_TOCHI22.pdf); [Readability Matters](https://readabilitymatters.org/?p=7025)
- CHI 2020 precursor (Wallace et al.): 63 participants aged 18–55, 16 fonts; Noto Sans the overall favourite; 77% believed their preferred font would be fastest but this held for only ~20% (another summary says 18%). — [Readability Matters](https://readabilitymatters.org/articles/individual-differences-in-font-preference-effectiveness); [Adobe blog](https://blog.adobe.com/en/publish/2022/05/10/the-need-to-personalize-fonts-for-each-individual-reader)
- Font recommendation (FontMART): Adobe reports that FontMART's recommended font sped readers by 14–25 wpm without hindering comprehension (venue/authors not confirmed in this session). — [Adobe readability page](https://www.adobe.com/se/corporate-responsibility/readability.html)
- Rulers: Niklaus et al. (CHI 2023) found no single ruler preferred by all; the fastest ruler varied across participants (dyslexia and non-dyslexia). — [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3544548.3581367)
- Dyslexia personalisation tooling: Rello & Baeza-Yates' DysWebxia/IDEAL eBook reader (ASSETS 2013) lets users adjust size/spacing/colours and on-demand synonyms; user studies of 47 participants reported higher readability/comprehensibility ratings. — [ASSETS 2013 PDF](https://www.changedyslexia.org/publications/pdfs/2013-ASSETS-An%20iOS%20Reader%20Dyslexia.pdf)
- Polarity individual differences: 2024 IEEE VIS short paper found the better polarity varied by individual. — [arXiv](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2409.10841)
- ADHD preference: interviews in the 2026 ACM pupillometry study highlight ADHD participants' preference for customizable interfaces. — [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/full/10.1145/3772363.3799383)

### Inferences
- The strongest personalisation evidence is that (a) individual optimum settings differ a lot and (b) self-report is a poor guide to the optimum; this argues for measured/adaptive recommendation (A/B within-user speed tests) rather than static "ADHD presets".
- Given ADHD readers' reported speed–comprehension trade-off, any adaptive system for ADHD should optimise comprehension-at-speed, not speed alone, which the Adobe metrics (speed with comprehension as a floor) partially address.

### Gaps
- No adaptive or personalised reading-interface study with ADHD participants and measured outcomes was found.
- The TOCHI DOI and the FontMART paper's citation (likely Cai, Wallace et al., DIS 2022) were not verified in this session.

## Cross-cutting classification (a)–(d) requested by the brief

### Takeaway
(a) ADHD-specific positive evidence: TTS/bimodal reading (two studies), keyword highlighting (n=3 single-case), added colour (Zentall, old/secondary), print over digital (2019), plus preference data for customisable distraction-free interfaces (2026). (b) Dyslexia/general-only evidence: letter spacing, larger font size, sans-serif/roman fonts, warm backgrounds, reading rulers, keyword bolding, lexical simplification, headings, light polarity, personalised fonts. (c) Null/negative: Bionic Reading, OpenDyslexic/Dyslexie letterforms, coloured overlays, Sans Forgetica, RSVP above normal speed, line spacing (Rello 2012), dark mode for proofreading. (d) Popular claims without controlled evidence: "ADHD fonts" (Lexend etc.), Bionic Reading for ADHD, BeeLine for ADHD, dark mode for ADHD focus, chunking/reader mode for ADHD, TTS "+25–30%".

### Cited Findings
- (a) ADHD-specific positive: TTS — [Chen, Hung & Jian, Reading and Writing](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11145-025-10738-5); [Swedish cross-over 2017](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0162643417742898). Highlighting — [Santos & Albuquerque 2021](https://periodicos.unb.br/index.php/revistaptp/article/view/22201/34195). Added colour — [NCEO summary of Zentall 2000](https://publications.ici.umn.edu/nceo/accommodations-toolkit/color-contrast-research). ### Inferences
- A defensible evidence-based ADHD reading product would: offer TTS/bimodal reading as a first-class feature; emphasise key terms; keep plain sans-serif fonts with generous size/spacing (dyslexia/general evidence, low risk); remove animated peripheral content; use light polarity by default with a toggle; let users personalise and measure rather than impose presets; avoid Bionic-style bolding and fixed-pace RSVP; and treat chunking/reader-mode as plausible-but-untested for ADHD.

### Gaps
- The absence of ADHD group-level trials for chunking, highlighting, simplification, rulers, dark mode and reader mode is the central gap; the report should say so explicitly rather than extrapolate from dyslexia.
