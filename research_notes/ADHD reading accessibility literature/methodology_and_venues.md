## Key question 1: Study design and outcome measures for reading-interface comparisons

### Takeaway
Published reading-interface comparisons with neurodivergent readers (mostly dyslexia; very few with ADHD adults) are overwhelmingly within-subject designs in which each participant reads several comparable passages, each in a different presentation condition, with reading speed (words per minute or passage time), eye-tracking metrics (fixation duration, saccades, pupil size) and multiple-choice comprehension questions as primary outcomes, plus subjective preference. ADHD-specific reading-interface evidence is thin (one 2026 CHI extended abstract with 6 ADHD participants; one 2024 online study with ~105 ADHD adults using thought probes), so a well-powered within-subject study with ASRS screening would fill a genuine gap.

### Cited Findings

Exemplar designs and measures

- Rello & Baeza-Yates, "Good Fonts for Dyslexia" (ASSETS 2013, DOI 10.1145/2513383.2513447): within-subject design, 48 participants with clinically confirmed dyslexia (ages 11–50) read 12 comparable texts each in a different font (Arial, Courier, Helvetica, OpenDyslexic, Times, Verdana, among others); eye tracking (fixation duration) measured reading performance and comprehension tests measured understanding; sans-serif, monospaced and roman fonts outperformed OpenDyslexic, and italics hurt reading. Counterbalancing of order was not confirmable from the snippets — check the full paper. — [a11y-paradise review summary](https://a11y-paradise.onrender.com/reviews/69d25a338a41f1818e3d54ca); [BYU Editing Research summary](https://editingresearch.byu.edu/2020/05/28/which-fonts-are-best-for-dyslexia/)
- Rello & Baeza-Yates, "How to present more readable text for people with dyslexia" (Universal Access in the Information Society, 2017, DOI 10.1007/s10209-015-0438-8): 92 participants (46 with dyslexia, 46 controls); manipulated colour, font size, column width, character/line/paragraph spacing; outcomes were eye-tracking reading measures plus preference questionnaires; larger text and larger character spacing made both groups read significantly faster. — [UPF repository record](https://repositori.upf.edu/items/e0ca6f40-a0fa-490f-9d03-53d043270deb)
- Rello et al. 2013 Wikipedia study (28 dyslexic participants): font size significantly affected readability and understandability, line spacing did not; recommends 18 pt for web text for dyslexic readers. — [UPF repository record](https://repositori.upf.edu/items/e0ca6f40-a0fa-490f-9d03-53d043270deb)
- A later critique of Rello's corpus notes that only half of participants could correctly identify which settings made them read faster, that readability and comprehension needed separate treatment, and that data from dyslexic participants "never showed normal distributions" (so plan non-parametric or mixed-model analyses). — [a11y-paradise review of "Dyslexia and web accessibility"](https://a11y-paradise.onrender.com/reviews/69bf2c89ad2e732db515c686)
- Wallace et al., "Towards Individuated Reading Experiences: Different Fonts Increase Reading Speed for Different Individuals" (ACM TOCHI, published 31 March 2022): large crowdsourced study of 16 fonts in adults reading during short gaps in the day; fonts were normalised by perceived size; headline result is a 35% difference in reading speed between each participant's fastest and slowest font with no change in comprehension; the best font differed per reader; materials and tools were released. — [Adobe Research page](https://research.adobe.com/publication/towards-individuated-reading-experiences-different-fonts-increase-reading-speed-for-different-individuals); [PDF (Jeff Huang)](https://jeffhuang.com/papers/Readability_TOCHI22.pdf); [Readability Matters summary](https://readabilitymatters.org/?p=7025)
- Llana, Baron, Yu, Hosseinpour, Suhail, Chin, Khadka & Wallace, "Reading with Diversity in Mind: Pupillometry and Typography Towards Inclusive Design for ADHD Readers" (CHI EA '26, DOI 10.1145/3772363.3799383 as listed by an aggregator; University of Rhode Island): in-lab eye-tracking study, 21 participants of whom 6 had ADHD, screened with the ASRS-v1.1; each participant read four eighth-grade-level passages, each randomly assigned one of four Roboto variants and one of three sizes, followed by five-question multiple-choice comprehension quizzes; outcomes were reading speed, comprehension, saccade counts and pupil dilation; reported findings: ADHD participants read faster (p = .029) with about 22% fewer saccades; faster reading correlated with smaller pupil dilation (r = -0.376, p < .001); a speed–accuracy trade-off across font variants; small font-size effects on comprehension; follow-up interviews with five ADHD participants; released an open-source stimulus generator "ReadGen". The DOI and author list come from a single aggregator page and were not verified against the ACM DL. — [a11y-paradise review](https://a11y-paradise.onrender.com/reviews/69ed2c3f083d4508fd230b16); preliminary 3-participant VSS 2025 poster: [visionsciences.org](https://visionsciences.org/presentation/?id=2470)
- Brann & Sidi (2024), "Metacognitive scaffolding for digital reading and mind-wandering" (Open University of Israel): 210 adults aged 20–50, about half with ADHD, online reading with thought probes; in the control condition the ADHD group had lower comprehension and lower confidence; with scaffolding the groups were comparable; state mind-wandering mediated the group comprehension gap only in the control condition. This is the best exemplar of thought-probe (mind-wandering) measurement in an ADHD reading study. — [Open University CRIS record](https://cris.openu.ac.il/en/publications/metacognitive-scaffolding-for-digital-reading-and-mind-wandering-/)
- Hurst (2020, University of Waterloo MA thesis), "On the Relationships Between Mind-Wandering, ADHD, Engagingness, and Effort": thought probes during textbook reading; ADHD symptoms predicted more inattention for unengaging texts but there was no evidence of an ADHD-symptom effect on comprehension. — [UWSpace record](https://uwspace.uwaterloo.ca/items/b6000f50-3ae8-4613-a6eb-19002513f983)
- Jonkman et al. (college students grouped by high/low inattention symptoms): the high-inattention group reported more mind-wandering during a reading task, associated with poorer text comprehension. — [PLoS ONE via IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/plo/pone00/0181213.html)
- Beymer, Russell & Orton (IBM) eye-tracking study, 82 participants: no significant serif vs sans-serif difference in online reading, serif slightly faster; standard reference for font-type null effects in non-ADHD adults. — [ScienceOpen PDF](https://www.scienceopen.com/document_file/671b2d53-73ce-4a91-946d-fde0b7db381a/ScienceOpen/015_Beymer.pdf); [IBM Research record](https://research.ibm.com/publications/an-eye-tracking-study-of-how-font-size-and-type-influence-online-reading)
- Jarosch, Schlesewsky & Füssel (2017), typography experiments with 32 participants reading for comprehension: font did not influence reading but full justification increased fixations, especially with wide spaces. — [IDS publication server PDF](https://ids-pub.bsz-bw.de/files/10274/Jarosch_Schlesewsky_Fuessel_Taking_typography_to_experimental_testing_2017.pdf)
- Ralekar et al. (IIT Jodhpur), Devanagari fonts, eye tracking with 14 participants across 3 fonts: significant differences in fixation duration, number of fixations and comprehension score. — [IIT Jodhpur record](https://research.iitj.ac.in/publication/effect-of-devanagari-font-type-in-reading-comprehension)
- Latin-square item assignment exemplar: the EMTeC eye-movement corpus assigned 42 items across 14 lists by Latin square so each participant saw each item once and each condition equally often, with item order randomised within list. — [arXiv 2408.04289](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.04289)
- A Utrecht master's thesis tested "Bionic Reading" with ADHD adults using EEG and reported inconclusive results. — [Utrecht theses record](https://studenttheses.uu.nl/handle/20.500.12932/47156)
- Text-to-speech/AI narration with ADHD adults: a 2025 qualitative (phenomenological) study in the Journal of Applied Instructional Design found TTS supported focus and comprehension, especially for complex texts; not a controlled comparison. — [EdTech Books](https://edtechbooks.org/jaid_14_2/oawranfbbj)

Subjective workload and usability instruments used with ADHD / dyslexia samples

- NASA-TLX has six subscales (mental, physical, temporal, performance, effort, frustration). — [Felton et al. 2012 PDF (UW ergonomics)](https://ergo.wisc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/901/2020/04/Felton-et-al-2012.pdf)
- NASA-TLX used with ADHD participants: a comparative study of people with and without ADHD recognising dark patterns on social media (arXiv 2503.05263, 2025) found dark patterns significantly affected total workload scores. — [arXiv 2503.05263](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.05263)
- NASA-TLX with dyslexic readers: a Pakistani web-accessibility study (n = 100, formally diagnosed dyslexia) reported lower NASA-TLX cognitive strain and shorter task time on accessibility-enhanced pages. — [Journal of Research in Psychology](https://jrp.lcwu.edu.pk/index.php/JRP/article/view/91)
- SUS with dyslexic learners: Alghabban & Hendley (2021) had 41 students with dyslexia rate an adaptive e-learning system with SUS. — [SciTePress](https://scitepress.org/PublishedPapers/2021/104528); SUS gives a 0–100 score and 70–100 is generally treated as acceptable. — [SciTePress 2024 paper](https://scitepress.org/publishedPapers/2024/123040/pdf/index.html)

Response bias and disability-centred methods (relevant to preference measures)

- Ming et al., "Accept or Address? Researchers' Perspectives on Response Bias in Accessibility Research" (ASSETS '21, DOI 10.1145/3441852.3471216): interviews with 27 accessibility researchers; participants sometimes downplayed difficulties to show competence, and feedback skewed positive because any improvement over existing technology felt like progress; some researchers consider labelling responses "biased" a form of epistemic violence; the paper gives practical guidelines organised by research question. — [a11y-paradise review](https://a11y-paradise.onrender.com/reviews/69b7f1ba1dd6cd9733e25dd2)
- McDonnell et al. (ASSETS '23) argue research processes frequently fail to centre disabled people or share power and propose a practical guide. — [a11y-paradise review](https://a11y-paradise.onrender.com/reviews/69b0aab768a1bab28c8d9f0a)

### Inferences
- The field standard for a reading-interface comparison is: within-subject, k conditions × k matched passages, assignment of passage-to-condition by Latin square, order randomised or counterbalanced, primary outcomes WPM (or passage time) and comprehension accuracy, secondary outcomes eye-tracking (fixation duration, fixation count, saccade amplitude/count, pupil size) and subjective preference; the CHI EA '26 ADHD study and the Rello corpus both fit this template.
- Given the Rello critique (non-normal distributions, preference not matching performance), analysis plans should prespecify linear mixed-effects models (participant and passage as random effects) and report effect sizes with confidence intervals (see Key question 3).
- Mind-wandering thought probes are an established, low-cost add-on for ADHD reading research (Brann & Sidi; Hurst; Jonkman) and would differentiate an ADHD study from generic typography work.
- Because ADHD participants in the CHI EA '26 study read faster with fewer saccades, speed alone is an ambiguous outcome for ADHD; pair it with comprehension and probe-caught mind-wandering.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve full texts, so I could not confirm whether Rello & Baeza-Yates 2013/2017 explicitly used Latin-square counterbalancing, nor the exact comprehension-question formats.
- No CHI/ASSETS full paper (as opposed to an extended abstract) reporting a within-subject reading-interface comparison specifically with ADHD adults was found.
- I found no ADHD reading-interface study reporting NASA-TLX or SUS; the NASA-TLX/SUS citations above are from adjacent ADHD or dyslexia studies.

## Key question 2: Participant screening and reporting for adult ADHD

### Takeaway
The ASRS v1.1 six-item screener (Kessler et al. 2005) and its DSM-5 successor (Ustun et al. 2017) are the standard self-report screens and are what the one published ADHD reading-typography HCI study used; CAARS (current symptoms) and WURS (retrospective childhood symptoms) are the usual complements. Self-report diagnosis carries documented false-positive risk, so papers should report both diagnosis source (self-report vs clinical) and screener scores. Accessibility venues expect respectful, specific disability language (SIGACCESS Accessible Writing Guide) and the median sample size in CHI/ASSETS accessibility studies with disabled participants is 13.

### Cited Findings

Screening instruments and psychometrics

- Kessler et al. (2005), "The World Health Organization adult ADHD Self-Report Scale (ASRS): a short screening scale for use in the general population", Psychological Medicine 35(2):245–256, DOI 10.1017/S0033291704002892: full scale has 18 DSM-IV Criterion A items; the 6-item screener was selected by stepwise logistic regression; validated against blind clinical ratings in 154 NCS-R respondents; the unweighted 6-item screener outperformed the 18-item scale on sensitivity (68.7% vs 56.3%), specificity (99.5% vs 98.3%), total classification accuracy (97.9% vs 96.2%) and kappa (0.76 vs 0.58); authors recommend the unweighted screener for community surveys and clinical case-finding. — [PubMed 15841682](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15841682/); [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/psychological-medicine/article/abs/world-health-organization-adult-adhd-selfreport-scale-asrs-a-short-screening-scale-for-use-in-the-general-population/28DF9AC948CE49D49B42AE9DABA325C1)
- Ustun et al. (2017), DSM-5 ASRS screener (JAMA Psychiatry; DOI 10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2017.0298 from memory, unverified): six items, four from DSM inattention/hyperactivity symptoms and two executive-dysfunction items; sensitivity 91.4% and specificity 96.0% in the NCS-R community sample, 91.9% and 74.0% in the NYU clinical sample; a 0–4 frequency scoring with a positive screen at 14 or higher (cutoff reported by a secondary source). — [ADHD in Adults blog summary](https://adhdinadults.com/adhd-screener/); [Turkish validation (PMC8909251)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8909251)
- Turkish ASRS-5 validation (68 adults with ADHD vs 68 controls, psychiatrist diagnosis as reference): AUC 0.916, sensitivity 85.2%, specificity 89.7% at a 9/10 cutoff. — [Koç University record](https://research.hub.ku.edu.tr/entities/publication/df39585e-1b75-4e53-8366-50946edfb819/full)
- German primary-care ASRS-5 validation (262 adults, structured IDA-R clinical interview as reference): sensitivity 95.6%, specificity 72.3%. — [LMU open-access record](https://oa-fund.ub.uni-muenchen.de/id/eprint/389)
- ASRS v1.1 in a treatment-seeking alcohol-dependent sample (PRISM reference): sensitivity 79.3% (95% CI 61.6–90.2), specificity 70.3% (95% CI 65.3–74.8) — shows specificity falls in comorbid samples. — [Journal of Attention Disorders](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/ref/10.1177/1087054716646450)
- A review of adult ADHD rating scales for research (medRxiv 2023) notes that CAARS and ASRS were built for clinical use but are widely used in research, and identifies the WHO ASRS as "the most promising" option; the WURS is a retrospective 61-item self-report of childhood symptoms, with earlier validation relying heavily on non-clinical samples; pairing a childhood-history measure with a current-symptom measure is common. — [medRxiv 2023.05.30.23290708](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2023.05.30.23290708.full.pdf); [PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/pmid/29486543)
- WURS psychometrics (J Psychiatr Res 2021): full WURS yielded five factors and discriminated ADHD from GAD/MDD better than the WURS-25; the short form discriminated ADHD from healthy controls better. — [PubMed 33493947](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33493947/)
- CAARS: long form has 66 items; self-report and observer forms share scales; German CAARS-S validation reported Cronbach's alpha .74–.95 and test-retest .85–.92; Catalan short forms supported a four-factor structure (Inattention/Memory, Hyperactivity/Restlessness, Impulsivity/Emotional Lability, Problems with Self-Concept), mean alpha .75; most CAARS subscales did not correlate with WURS (they measure different constructs). — [European Psychiatry (Cambridge)](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/european-psychiatry/article/abs/german-validation-of-the-conners-adult-adhd-rating-scales-caars-ii-reliability-validity-diagnostic-sensitivity-and-specificity/156D836F77944AFB142AC934828FD8EC); [UB repository](https://diposit.ub.edu/items/35f54aab-712a-4e2e-9525-26bff8530fa3/full)

Self-reported diagnosis vs clinical confirmation

- A 2025 scoping review, "Diagnosing ADHD in adults in randomized controlled studies", cites a meta-analysis showing self-report diagnostic methods "often yielded false-positive diagnoses", and sole reliance on self-report was linked to low diagnostic-persistence estimates. — [PMC12188335](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12188335/)
- A 2015 study of 6,526 Mechanical Turk workers found the prevalence of self-reported childhood and adult ADHD diagnoses matched offline samples and concluded MTurk "holds promise as a recruitment tool for studying adults with ADHD". — [Peeref record](https://peeref.com/works/735188)
- A 2025 Journal of Attention Disorders study estimated 13.9% (95% CI 13.0–15.0%) of US working-age adults self-report ever being diagnosed with ADHD by a health care professional, attributing the rise partly to over- and mis-diagnosis and methodological issues. — [Syracuse Maxwell School summary](https://www.maxwell.syr.edu/research/article/self-reported-adhd-diagnosis-status-among-working-age-adults-in-the-united-states)
- Exemplar HCI practice: the CHI EA '26 pupillometry study screened with ASRS-v1.1 rather than requiring documentation of a clinical diagnosis (6 of 21 participants classified as ADHD). — [a11y-paradise review](https://a11y-paradise.onrender.com/reviews/69ed2c3f083d4508fd230b16)

ADHD-specific HCI literature on framing and sample reporting

- Spiel, Hornecker, Williams & Good, "ADHD and Technology Research – Investigated by Neurodivergent Readers" (CHI '22): critical review finding most ADHD technology work aims to mitigate ADHD traits, few projects involve people with ADHD as co-designers, and participant resistance to deficit framing is visible in researchers' own accounts. — [UvA DARE record](https://dare.uva.nl/id/b5158a6b-ee52-48a7-b4be-772d430bad4a); [ResearchGate PDF](https://researchgate.net/profile/Katta-Spiel/publication/360330913_ADHD_and_Technology_Research_-_Investigated_by_Neurodivergent_Readers/links/6282c0e27a08f263d551e17c/ADHD-and-Technology-Research-Investigated-by-Neurodivergent-Readers.pdf)
- A systematic review of supportive technologies for ADHD in HCI (arXiv 2005.09993) found most studies report age and gender but none report race, socioeconomic data was missing, and only three studies had control groups. — [arXiv 2005.09993](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2005.09993)
- Stefanidi et al. (IDC '22) found most HCI contributions target ADHD symptoms and rarely involve the care ecosystem. — [UCL Discovery](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10153234/)
- Burtscher & Gerling (CHIWORK '24) reviewed 11 HCI papers on neurodivergence and work and found a fragmented landscape. — [ACM DL via Unpaywall](https://unpaywall.org/10.1145%2F3663384.3663386)

### Inferences
- Recommended reporting for an ADHD adult sample: (a) diagnosis source (clinician-confirmed vs self-reported; age at diagnosis; medication status on test day), (b) ASRS v1.1 or ASRS-5 score for every participant including controls, (c) optionally CAARS-S for current symptom severity and WURS-25 for childhood history, (d) comorbidities (dyslexia, anxiety) because they affect specificity, (e) accommodations offered.
- Given screener specificity of ~70–75% in clinical/comorbid samples, a self-report-only ADHD group should be described as "adults with self-reported ADHD diagnosis who screened positive on ASRS" rather than as a clinically diagnosed group.
- Spiel et al. and McDonnell et al. imply reviewers at CHI/ASSETS will look for non-deficit framing and some participatory input (e.g., ADHD readers consulted on stimuli or interface design).

### Gaps
- I could not locate a single official "Guidelines for research with people with disabilities" document from SIGACCESS or SIGCHI under that title; the closest official resource is the SIGACCESS Accessible Writing Guide. If such a document exists it was not surfaced.
- Caine-style statistics on typical sample sizes specifically for ADHD HCI studies were not found; only the Mack et al. median (13) for accessibility studies generally and the ADHD review's note on missing demographics.
- Exact Ustun 2017 cutoff and DOI were not verified against the original JAMA Psychiatry paper.

## Key question 3: Power and sample size

### Takeaway
The most common CHI sample size is 12 (Caine 2016) and the median accessibility-study sample is 13 (Mack et al. 2021); power analyses are rare at CHI and effect sizes under-reported. HCI methodologists (Dragicevic; Cockburn et al.) recommend estimation (effect sizes with CIs) and preregistration; for a within-subject reading design, power can be computed with G*Power's paired-samples t-test using a Cohen's d from prior typography work and a conservative within-subject correlation.

### Cited Findings
- Caine, "Local Standards for Sample Size at CHI" (CHI '16, pp. 981–992, DOI 10.1145/2858036.2858498): analysed all CHI 2014 manuscripts; sample sizes ranged from 1 to 916,000; the most common sample size was 12; sample size differed by setting and methodology. The median was not visible in snippets. — [Clemson repository](https://open.clemson.edu/computing_pubs/28); [dblp](https://dblp1.uni-trier.de/rec/conf/chi/Caine16.html)
- Mack et al. (CHI 2021): median participant-group size including people with disabilities/older adults is 13 in CHI/ASSETS accessibility papers 2010–2019. — [arXiv 2101.04271](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2101.04271)
- Dragicevic, "Fair Statistical Communication in HCI", Chapter 13 of Robertson & Kaptein (eds), Modern Statistical Methods for HCI, Springer 2016, pp. 291–330, ISBN 978-3-319-26631-2 (chapter DOI 10.1007/978-3-319-26633-6_13 from memory, unverified): argues for moving from NHST to estimation (effect sizes and CIs); errata note that in typical simple HCI analyses CIs often approximate credible intervals with uninformed priors. — [LISN publication record](https://www.lisn.upsaclay.fr/publications/fair-statistical-communication-in-hci/); [Springer Professional](https://www.springerprofessional.de/en/fair-statistical-communication-in-hci/7840092); [author errata](https://aviz.fr/estimationreport)
- Dragicevic et al., "HCI Statistics without p-values" (INRIA research report 2015): explains why HCI should switch from significance testing to "informative charts with effect sizes and confidence intervals". — [aviz.fr PDF](https://aviz.fr/wiki/uploads/Research/HCI-stats-without-p-values.pdf)
- A University of Bonn lab seminar project surveys how effect size is reported in CHI publications (status-quo review; useful lead for a citation on under-reporting). — [besec.uni-bonn.de](https://besec.uni-bonn.de/course/ss24labpgsem/reporting_status_lab/)
- Power for within-subject designs: a 2×2 within-subject design can be reduced to a paired-samples t-test, so power only needs an expected Cohen's d rather than partial eta-squared; whether G*Power handles within-subject interactions directly or needs simulation is questioned. — [PMC10439102](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10439102)
- G*Power paired-samples setup, including choosing the correlation between measures (which strongly affects required N). — [UCLA OARC guide](https://stats.oarc.ucla.edu/gpower/power-analysis-for-paired-sample-t-test/)
- "Tutorial: Small-N Power Analysis" explains converting just-noticeable differences into standardised effect sizes for G*Power. — [UNE repository](https://rune.une.edu.au/web/handle/1959.11/54668)
- Defaults recommended for HRI/HCI when no prior estimate exists: medium effect size, alpha .05, power .80 for in-person studies (.90–.95 online). — [UChicago HRI wiki](https://hri.cs.uchicago.edu/pages/wiki-entries/conducting-statistical-analyses-hri-data.html)
- A 2026 paper introduces "PowerMeter", a power-analysis tool for HCI researchers motivated by G*Power's difficulty. — [Fraunhofer Publica](https://publica.fraunhofer.de/handle/publica/516658)
- Arif, "Effect Size, Sample Size, & Statistical Power in HCI Research" (tutorial notes). — [asarif.com](https://asarif.com/notes/EffectSamplePower.html)
- Effect-size inputs from the literature: Wallace et al. 2022 report a 35% within-person reading-speed range across 16 fonts (TOCHI); Rello & Baeza-Yates 2017 report significant speed gains for larger size/spacing in 92 participants. — [Adobe Research](https://research.adobe.com/publication/towards-individuated-reading-experiences-different-fonts-increase-reading-speed-for-different-individuals); [UPF](https://repositori.upf.edu/items/e0ca6f40-a0fa-490f-9d03-53d043270deb)

### Inferences
- A reading-interface study with 24–36 ADHD adults (2–3× the field median) in a fully within-subject design, with a preregistered mixed-model analysis and an a priori G*Power calculation (paired t, d from pilot, r ≈ 0.5), would exceed local CHI/ASSETS norms and answer the standard "underpowered" reviewer critique.
- Reviewers familiar with Dragicevic will expect estimation plots (means with 95% CIs) rather than bare p-values.

### Gaps
- Caine 2016's median sample size and the proportion of CHI 2014 papers reporting a power analysis were not visible in snippets (the paper is paywalled and fetch was blocked).

## Key question 4: Ethics approval for independent/industry researchers (US and EU) and GDPR

### Takeaway
Neither the US Common Rule nor EU law makes ethics review automatically mandatory for a privately funded, non-institutional behavioural study, but CHI, ASSETS and the journals all require an ethics statement, and the practical routes for an independent or company researcher are a commercial/independent IRB (US: e.g., WCG, Advarra; fees in the thousands of dollars), a university partner IRB, or a national independent ethics committee in the EU. In the EU, an ADHD diagnosis is "data concerning health" under GDPR Article 9, so processing needs an Article 6 basis plus an Article 9(2) condition (explicit consent 9(2)(a) or research 9(2)(j) with Article 89 safeguards such as pseudonymisation).

### Cited Findings

Venue expectations

- CHI 2026 Papers author page: studies involving people need ethics review "under the requirements of the authors' research environment", and authors should add a short note to reviewers explaining that context. — [chi2026.acm.org/authors/papers/](https://chi2026.acm.org/authors/papers/)
- Journals: "for most indexed journals, it is mandatory for every submission to include an ethical approval statement if the study involves animals or human participants. Being an independent researcher does not change this requirement." — [Editage Insights](https://www.editage.com/insights/how-do-i-get-an-ethical-approval-without-being-affiliated-with-any-institute)
- COPE case guidance exists for "studies where there is no research ethics committee or where committees disagree". — [COPE case](https://publicationethics.org/guidance/case/studies-where-there-no-research-ethics-committee-or-where-committees-disagree-need-approval)
- Historical note: a Toronto HCI lab mailing-list post reports CHI asking authors to state whether the protocol was approved by an ethics board, with a suggested methods-section sentence; anecdotal and possibly dated. — [DGP thirdspace archive](https://www.dgp.toronto.edu/mailman/archives/list/thirdspace@dgp.toronto.edu/message/MP4HBMGBKCTRYFBRTKQ5RRUXXEA52LD6)

United States (45 CFR 46, the Common Rule)

- Scope: the Federalwide Assurance terms "apply whenever the Institution becomes engaged in human subjects research conducted or supported by any U.S. federal department or agency that has adopted the Common Rule"; "federally-supported means the U.S. Government providing any funding or other support." The FWA commits the institution, not an individual investigator, and must designate an OHRP-registered IRB. — [OHRP FWA terms PDF (hhs.gov)](https://www.hhs.gov/ohrp/sites/default/files/ohrp/documents/terms092310rev.pdf); [CASRAI FWA entry](https://www.casrai.org/dictionary/term/federalwide-assurance-fwa)
- The revised Common Rule was published January 2017 and took effect in 2018; exemptions moved to 46.104 with eight categories in 46.104(d). — [CITI Program summary](https://about.citiprogram.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/Final-Rule-Material-Changes-to-Exempt-Determination-Process.pdf)
- Exemption 46.104(d)(3), "benign behavioral interventions": covers "research involving benign behavioral interventions in conjunction with the collection of information from an adult subject through verbal or written responses (including data entry) or audiovisual recording" with prospective agreement; interventions must be "brief in duration, harmless, painless, not physically invasive, not likely to have a significant adverse lasting impact on the subjects" and not offensive or embarrassing; examples include playing an online game or solving puzzles under various noise conditions; not usable with children; if identifiable, limited IRB review is required. — [Pitt HRPO](https://www.hrpo.pitt.edu/node/903); [HHS "ABCs of 104" slides](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/abcs-of-104-understanding-exemption-categories-slides.pdf)
- Independent/commercial IRBs are the main route for entities without academic affiliation (Advarra named as an example); some university IRBs will review outside research (Johns Hopkins BIOS cited), while others (Fordham) require unaffiliated researchers to have their own institution's approval first; social/behavioural researchers without an affiliated IRB "may need a private IRB"; CASRAI cites WCG's 2026 initial review fee as $5,900 but notes commercial IRB pricing is largely negotiated. — [CASRAI IRB cost guide](https://www.casrai.org/guides/irb-approval-cost); [SAGE Research Methods Community](https://researchmethodscommunity.sagepub.com/blog/independent-research-and-irb-reviews); [Fordham IRB](https://www.fordham.edu/academics/research/institutional-review-board/guidelines-and-procedures/research-conducted-by-researchers-not-affiliated-with-fordham-university/); [UW–Madison IRB fee schedule](https://irb.wisc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/sites/2/2024/06/IRBFeeschedule-5-2024.pdf)
- University of Wisconsin policy: fully industry-sponsored studies are reviewed by an independent IRB except for limited exceptions. — [UW–Madison IRB](https://irb.wisc.edu/2024/06/20/update-to-considerations-for-industry-funded-studies-reviewed-locally/)

European Union / UK

- Options for independent researchers: "explore independent ethics committees (IECs) in your country", or "contact nearby universities or institutions that have an ethics review board and inquire if they are willing to oversee ethical aspects of your study." — [Editage Insights](https://www.editage.com/insights/how-do-i-get-an-ethical-approval-without-being-affiliated-with-any-institute)
- EU-funded work: independent researchers without formal ethics approval must describe potential ethical issues using the European Commission's ethics self-assessment, which an ethics panel may review. — [UOW guidance](https://ro.uow.edu.au/test2021/2129)
- Ethics review requirements for low-risk non-interventional studies "vary enormously across European countries". — [PubMed 31159853](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31159853/)

GDPR and special-category data

- Article 9(1): "Processing of personal data revealing racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs, or trade union membership, and the processing of genetic data, biometric data for the purpose of uniquely identifying a natural person, data concerning health or data concerning a natural person's sex life or sexual orientation shall be prohibited." Article 9(2): "Paragraph 1 shall not apply if one of the following applies" (list of conditions including explicit consent and scientific research). Recital 51 explains the heightened protection; Recital 53 limits health-data processing to specified purposes. — [gdpr-text.com Article 9](https://gdpr-text.com/en/read/article-9/)
- Health research needs both an Article 6 basis and an Article 9 exception; researchers may rely on Article 6(1)(e) or (f) with Article 9(2)(j) or (i); EDPB 2021 health-research material: ethics standards "cannot be interpreted in such a way that only explicit consent of data subjects can be used to legitimise the processing of health data for scientific research purposes", and research-participation consent is distinct from GDPR consent as a legal basis. — [EDPB 2021 response on GDPR and health research (via EJP RD)](https://www.ejprarediseases.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/edpb_application-of-the-GDPR-on-health-research_2021.pdf)
- EDPB COVID-19 research guidelines: explicit consent is acceptable but requires caution because "the data subject must not be pressured into providing their consent". — [A&L Goodbody summary](https://www.algoodbody.com/insights-publications/edpb-publishes-guidelines-on-processing-health-data-for-covid-19-research)
- Article 89(1) safeguards require technical and organisational measures ensuring data minimisation, with pseudonymisation named. — [Legiscope summary](https://www.legiscope.com/blog/health-data-article-9-gdpr.html)
- Recital 33 allows consent to broadly defined research areas, read narrowly by supervisory authorities. — [Hogan Lovells summary of EDPB research guidelines](https://www.hoganlovells.com/en/publications/edpb-guidelines-on-processing-personal-data-for-scientific-research-key-takeaways)
- EDPB draft Guidelines 1/2026 on processing personal data for scientific research were under consultation in 2026 (EFPIA response filed); they stress considering participants' mental or physical condition when judging whether consent is freely given; final adoption status not confirmed. — [EFPIA consultation response (edpb.europa.eu)](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/consultation_feedback/1481/feedback_1481_efpia-response-to-the-edpb-consultation-on-guidelines-1_2026.pdf); [JD Supra summary](https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/edpb-issues-draft-guidelines-for-use-of-9046036/)

### Inferences
- For a US-based independent researcher with no federal funding, 45 CFR 46 does not legally apply, but a reading study with ADHD adults would plausibly fit the 46.104(d)(3) "benign behavioral intervention" exemption; obtaining an exempt determination or expedited approval from a commercial IRB (budget roughly US$2,000–6,000 based on the WCG figure) or a partner university IRB produces the statement CHI/ASSETS/journals expect.
- For an EU-based researcher, the practical compliance set is: a documented legal basis (Article 6(1)(a) consent or (f) legitimate interests) plus Article 9(2)(a) explicit consent for the ADHD-status field, a data-protection impact assessment if eye-tracking or large-scale health data are involved, pseudonymised storage, and ethics review by a university partner or national independent committee; the EDPB notes that consent to participate and GDPR consent are separate documents/clauses.
- Reviewers will accept "exempt" or "reviewed by an independent IRB" if named explicitly; an unreviewed study must at least explain the environment per the CHI 2026 note.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve OHRP's own text on applicability (46.101) or its guidance on "engagement" for unaffiliated investigators.
- No source was found that lists specific commercial ethics committees in EU countries, their fees, or their recognition by journals.
- The EUR-Lex official text of Article 9(2)(a) and (j) could not be fetched; wording for the exceptions is from secondary summaries.

## Key question 5: Preregistration and open science

### Takeaway
Preregistration is encouraged but rare in HCI: Cockburn, Gutwin & Dix (CHI 2018) made the case, and Pang, Reinecke & Just (CHI 2022) found only 1.11% of papers in four CHI proceedings were preregistered. OSF offers the general OSF Preregistration template, the AsPredicted 8-question template and a social-psychology template suited to human-subject experiments; Registered Report templates are for journals that offer in-principle acceptance.

### Cited Findings
- Cockburn, Gutwin & Dix, "HARK No More: On the Preregistration of CHI Experiments" (CHI 2018): preregistration helps reviewers trust that evidence "is not the product of hypothesising after results are known"; reviews preregistration across disciplines and adapts it to HCI. — [Cardiff Met record](https://pure.cardiffmet.ac.uk/en/publications/hark-no-more-on-the-preregistration-of-chi-experiments/); [FORRT entry](https://forrt.org/curated_resources/hark-no-more-on-the-preregistration-of-c/)
- Pang, Reinecke & Just, "Apéritif: Scaffolding Preregistrations to Automatically Generate Analysis Code and Methods Descriptions" (CHI 2022, DOI 10.1145/3491102.3517707): only 1.11% of papers in the past four CHI proceedings presented preregistered studies; authors and reviewers of such studies saw it as beneficial; a study with 17 HCI researchers found Apéritif reduced time to preregister compared with the AsPredicted template. — [PDF (UW)](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~rjust/publ/scaffolding_preregistration_chi_2022.pdf)
- OSF templates: AsPredicted template is "eight questions derived from content recommended by AsPredicted.org"; the OSF Preregistration template asks for more detail on sample-size justification; the "Pre-Registration in Social Psychology" template emphasises hypotheses, methods, analysis plans, variables and inclusion/exclusion criteria; the Registered Report Protocol template is for use after in-principle acceptance. — [OSF Help: Select a Registration Template](https://help.osf.io/hc/en-us/articles/360019738794-Select-a-Registration-Template); [COS blog](https://www.cos.io/blog/choosing-preregistration-template-guide-for-researchers)
- AsPredicted preregistration template (Word form). — [Pomona College copy](https://www.pomona.edu/sites/default/files/aspredicted-preregistration-template.docx)
- ADHD-HCI exemplar of registration: a PROSPERO-registered review of ADHD apps defined its population as adults and children diagnosed with ADHD. — [JMIR Mental Health preprint](https://preprints.jmir.org/preprint/51459)

### Inferences
- Preregistering on OSF (social-psychology or general template) with the analysis code and stimuli released, as Wallace et al. 2022 and the CHI EA '26 ADHD study did, would be a visible differentiator at CHI/ASSETS given the 1.11% base rate.

### Gaps
- I found no explicit statement on CHI 2026 or ASSETS 2025/2026 pages that preregistration is formally encouraged or rewarded; the evidence is from the methodology literature, not the author guides.

## Key question 7: AI-use (generative AI / LLM) policies at venues and publishers, 2025–2026

### Takeaway
All relevant publishers prohibit AI authorship and require disclosure of generative-AI use beyond copy-editing: ACM (policy of April 2023; "must be fully disclosed in the Work"), CHI 2026 (disclosure must be visible in the anonymised submission because the LaTeX template hides Acknowledgements in review mode; undisclosed use is a desk-reject ground), IEEE (disclose in the acknowledgments section), Elsevier (a titled declaration above the references), Springer Nature (document in Methods; copy-editing exempt), Taylor & Francis (statement with tool name, version, how and why), COPE (Methods disclosure) and arXiv (report significant use; AI cannot be an author). Dates: ACM April 2023; arXiv January 2023; COPE 13 February 2023; T&F 12 June 2024 (per CASRAI); CHI 2026 page current as of late 2025.

### Cited Findings
- ACM Policy on Authorship (April 2023 update): "The use of generative AI tools and technologies to create content is permitted but must be fully disclosed in the Work"; "Generative AI tools and technologies, such as ChatGPT, may not be listed as authors of an ACM published Work"; "Basic word processing systems are to be considered exceptions to this disclosure requirement"; authors are responsible for plagiarism, fabrication or falsification introduced by AI tools and can be sanctioned (e.g., publication ban); basic spell/grammar check and language translation are exempt. — [SIGCHI blog: ACM Publications Policy guidance for SIGCHI venues](https://sigchi.org/news/blog/acm-publications-policy-guidance-for-sigchi-venues/); [SIGCSE 2025 policy page quoting ACM](https://sigcse2025.sigcse.org/info/policies-ai); [CASRAI ACM authorship entry](https://www.casrai.org/dictionary/term/acm-policy-on-authorship)
- SIGCHI's guidance: AI tools "may be safely used to copy-edit author-generated content, but caution is advised in using them for generating content." — [SIGCHI blog](https://sigchi.org/news/blog/acm-publications-policy-guidance-for-sigchi-venues/)
- ACM-affiliated venues additionally require: reviewers may not use generative AI to write reviews or upload submissions to third-party LLMs; authors using copyrighted material as LLM input/training data must disclose permission in acknowledgements. — [RESPECT 2026 policy page](https://respect.acm.org/2026/?p=119)
- CHI 2026 Papers: generated text must be disclosed when a tool does more than edit the authors' own writing; the committee "will not use tools on a large scale to detect LLM-generated text, but will investigate flagged submissions and desk-reject papers where LLM use is not clearly marked"; the LaTeX template hides the Acknowledgements section in review mode, so the disclosure must be placed where it stays visible in the submitted version; authors are pointed to the April 2023 ACM Policy on Authorship and the SIGCHI blog post; violating the LLM policy is a listed desk-reject criterion, and the 2026 desk-reject insights post groups "unjustified/undisclosed LLM use" under miscellaneous desk rejects. — [CHI 2026 Papers](https://chi2026.acm.org/authors/papers/); [CHI 2026 desk-reject process](https://chi2026.acm.org/2025/08/08/revised-chi-2026-papers-desk-reject-process/); [CHI 2026 insights post](https://chi2026.acm.org/?p=1418)
- Survey of CS venue practice: "Expectations and Practices around AI Disclosure in CS Research" (arXiv 2608.23271, 2026) and "Generative AI Policies under the Microscope" (arXiv 2410.11977, examined 64 CS conferences) document the disclosure landscape; USENIX requires a separate disclosure section addressing limitations AI introduced. — [arXiv 2608.23271](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.23271); [arXiv 2410.11977](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.11977v4); [USENIX policy](https://www.usenix.org/node/316785)
- IEEE (PSPB Operations Manual 8.2.1.B): "The use of content generated by artificial intelligence (AI) in an article (including but not limited to text, figures, images, and code) shall be disclosed in the acknowledgments section"; 8.2.1.B.10: "The use of AI systems for editing and grammar enhancement is common practice and, as such, is generally outside the intent of the above policy. In this case, disclosure as noted above is recommended."; 8.2.1.C.6 bars reviewers from uploading manuscripts to public AI platforms ("considered a breach of confidentiality"). — [IEEE PELS information for authors (reproducing IEEE policy)](https://www.ieee-pels.org/publications/information-for-authors/); [Auckland LibGuide on IEEE generative AI](https://auckland.libguides.com/ieee/generative-ai); [CASRAI IEEE entry](https://casrai.org/dictionary/term/ieee-generative-ai-policy)
- Elsevier: statement "should be inserted at the end of the manuscript, immediately above the references, entitled 'Declaration of Generative AI and AI-assisted technologies in the writing process.'" Template: "During the preparation of this work the author(s) used [NAME TOOL / SERVICE] in order to [REASON]. After using this tool/service, the author(s) reviewed and edited the content as needed and take(s) full responsibility for the content of the publication." Basic grammar/spelling/punctuation checks need no declaration; the policy covers writing only, and "AI use in the research process should be declared and described in detail in the methods section, if relevant." AI cannot be an author. — [Elsevier generative AI policy for books](https://www.elsevier.com/about/policies-and-standards/publishing-ethics-books/generative-ai-policies); [Elsevier writing policy page](https://www.elsevier.com/en-gb/about/policies-and-standards/the-use-of-generative-ai-and-ai-assisted-technologies-in-writing-for-elsevier); template text as reproduced by [WCN/ISN](https://www.theisn.org/wcn/wp-content/uploads/sites/8/2025/05/WCN-Declaration-of-generative-AI-in-scientific-writing.pdf). (Applies to IJHCS, which is an Elsevier journal.)
- Springer Nature (editorial policy on AI): "Large Language Models (LLMs), such as ChatGPT, do not currently satisfy our authorship criteria" because "an attribution of authorship carries with it accountability for the work, which cannot be effectively applied to LLMs"; LLM use should be documented in the Methods section (or a suitable alternative where there is no Methods section); "AI assisted copy editing" (improvements to human-generated text for readability, style and error checking) need not be declared; AI-generated images are not permitted (per third-party summaries); policy is monitored and will be updated. — [Springer editorial policy on AI (US mirror)](https://WWW.SPRINGER.COM/us/editorial-policies/artificial-intelligence--ai-/25428500); [Springer Nature AI guidance](https://group.springernature.com/br/group/ai/ai-guidance-for-our-researchers-and-communities); AI-image ban and 3,000-journal scope from [Manusights summary](https://manusights.com/blog/nature-ai-policy). (Applies to Universal Access in the Information Society.)
- Taylor & Francis AI policy: authors "must clearly acknowledge any use of Generative AI tools through a statement that includes the full tool name with version number, how it was used, and the reason for use"; "a specific statement of AI usage disclosure must be included" for article submissions; authorship is human-only because accountability, consent and contractual assurances "are uniquely human responsibilities"; CASRAI dates the policy to 12 June 2024. — [Taylor & Francis AI policy](https://taylorandfrancis.com/our-policies/ai-policy/); [CASRAI T&F guide](https://casrai.org/guides/taylor-francis-generative-ai-authorship-policy). (Applies to Behaviour & Information Technology.)
- COPE position statement "Authorship and AI tools" (first published 13 February 2023): AI tools cannot be listed as authors because they "cannot take responsibility for the submitted work", cannot declare conflicts of interest or manage copyright; authors using AI for writing, figures, or data collection/analysis should state in the Methods which tool was used and how; humans remain fully accountable. — [COPE position statement](https://publicationethics.org/guidance/cope-position/authorship-and-ai-tools)
- arXiv (policy announced 31 January 2023): generative AI language tools cannot be authors because "a computer program cannot take responsibility for a paper's contents" or agree to arXiv's terms; authors must report significant use of sophisticated tools including text-to-text generative AI "in line with subject methodology standards"; the current wording lives on arXiv's moderation/policy page, which could not be fetched. — [arXiv blog announcement](https://blog.arxiv.org/2023/01/31/arxiv-announces-new-policy-on-chatgpt-and-similar-tools)
- Practical placement guidance: Springer and Wiley use Methods or Acknowledgements; Elsevier uses a separate declaration; disclosures should name the tool, version, use and reason, and "if you're unsure, disclosing is the safer choice". — [Kennesaw State AI disclosure guide](https://www.kennesaw.edu/research/ai-disclosure.php); [CASRAI "what to declare and where"](https://casrai.org/news/disclosing-generative-ai-use-what-to-declare-and-where)

### Inferences
- A single disclosure paragraph that names the tool and version, what it was used for (e.g., copy-editing, code assistance, figure captions), that authors reviewed all output and take responsibility, placed (a) in the body for the anonymised CHI/ASSETS submission and (b) in Acknowledgements for camera-ready, satisfies ACM, CHI 2026 and IEEE; for Elsevier/T&F/Springer journals the same text goes under the publisher's required heading or in Methods.
- Use of an LLM to analyse participant data (e.g., coding interview transcripts) must go in Methods under COPE, Elsevier and Springer Nature rules, not just in Acknowledgements.
